## What techniques exist for generating the joint/correlated distribution of macro-fiscal shocks (copulas, Bayesian VAR, regime-switching, ML scenario generation) beyond a simple VAR or independent parameter sampling?

### Takeaway
Three distinct strands go beyond simple VAR/independent sampling: (1) vine-copula dependence modeling of sovereign stress across countries, (2) Bayesian VAR with informative priors plus a post-hoc "tilting" step toward a baseline (used operationally at the ESM and in a 2025 Banque de France paper), and (3) stochastic optimization frameworks (Consiglio & Zenios and co-authors) that treat correlated risk factors explicitly and optimize debt management against tail-risk measures (CVaR-type "Debt-at-Risk"/"Flow-at-Risk") rather than just reporting percentiles.

### Cited Findings
- Zhang, Dalu (2014), "Vine copulas and applications to the European Union sovereign debt analysis," *International Review of Financial Analysis*, vol. 36, pp. 46–56 (DOI: 10.1016/j.irfa.2014.02.011). Uses a GARCH-based vine-copula simulation technique to model the dependence structure (including tail dependence) of sovereign bond spreads over Germany across 11 EU countries, estimating future sovereign-crisis probability; finds Italy and Spain the most likely to face a next sovereign debt crisis, followed by Ireland, France and Belgium, with the UK, Sweden and Denmark most stable — [Source](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v36y2014icp46-56.html) (abstract-only read, via search synthesis, not the full paper).
- Consiglio, A. & Zenios, S.A. (2017), "Stochastic debt sustainability analysis for sovereigns," *Optimization Engineering* (DOI: 10.1007/s11081-017-9360-7). Argues sovereign DSA needs stochastic *correlated* risk factors plus a tail-sensitive risk measure (not just percentile reporting), illustrated with a stylized "Kingdom of Atlantis" case — [Source](https://iris.unipa.it/bitstream/10447/243631/1/10.1007%40s11081-017-9360-7.pdf) (abstract-only read).
- Consiglio & Zenios (2015), "Risk Management Optimization for Sovereign Debt Restructuring," *Journal of Globalization and Development*. Proposes a multi-period stochastic programming model minimizing expected financing cost subject to risk limits, defining a tail-risk measure called "conditional Debt-at-Risk," applied to the Greek debt crisis — [Source](https://gnosis.library.ucy.ac.cy/handle/7/46103) (abstract-only read).
- Consiglio, Zenios, and ESM co-authors (2018), ESM Working Paper No. 31, later published in *Operations Research* (2021) per the SSRN listing; a parallel version circulated as Dallas Fed Working Paper 367. Optimizes sovereign debt maturity structure to trade off borrowing cost against refinancing risk using a "conditional Flow-at-Risk" tail measure; described as "an essential building block of the ESM's methodological framework" — [Source](https://www.esm.europa.eu/system/files/document/wp-31final.pdf); [Source](https://www.dallasfed.org/~/media/documents/institute/wpapers/2019/0367.pdf) (abstract-only read for both).
- Ajovalasit, Consiglio, Pagliardi & Zenios (2025), eabh Paper No. 25-01, "Is political risk a threat to sovereign debt sustainability?" Extends the stochastic DSA optimization model to include political-risk channels; finds political risk can render debt unsustainable and that models ignoring it misclassify sustainable debt as unsustainable — [Source](https://bankinghistory.org/wp-content/uploads/eabhPaper_202501.pdf) (abstract-only read).
- Hitaj, Zavalloni & Zigraiova (2025), "Debt sustainability analysis under uncertainty: mapping the past into the future," ESM Briefs 05/2025, December 2025. Full text read. The ESM's operational fan-chart methodology uses a quarterly **Bayesian VAR with 7 variables** (real GDP growth, GDP deflator growth, primary surplus, 3-month Euribor, 10-year German Bund yield, and the long-term spread to the Bund) with **informative priors** on long-run behavior (similar to Villani, 2007) built from the European Commission's Ageing Report, historical averages, and country-economist judgment. Critically, it does **not** stop at the independent BVAR draw: in a second step the BVAR-simulated distribution for each debt driver is "tilted" toward the deterministic baseline, producing deliberately **asymmetric** fan charts whose skew reflects the gap between model and expert-judgment baseline — a distinct, named alternative to copula modeling for inducing realistic dependence/skew — [Source](https://www.esm.europa.eu/system/files/document/2025-11/ESM%20Brief%205%20-%20Debt%20sustainability%20under%20uncertainty.pdf) (full text read).
- Baret, Bec & Cochard (2025) [author names per search-engine synthesis, not independently verified from the full PDF], "Quantifying Uncertainty in France's Debt Trajectory: A VAR-Based Analysis," Banque de France Working Paper No. 1019 (November 2025). Estimates both frequentist and Bayesian VARs on French quarterly data 1990–2023 to produce debt-to-GDP fan charts; finds the Bayesian version produces wider uncertainty bands than the frequentist one, with band widths falling between those reported by the European Commission and the ECB — [Source](https://www.banque-france.fr/system/files/2025-11/WP1019_0.pdf) (search-snippet read only; the PDF's text layer could not be extracted before tool access was cut off by a session limit, so author names and methodological details beyond this are unverified and should be treated cautiously).
- Hollmayr (Bundesbank Discussion Paper), fiscal sustainability with Markov-switching active/passive fiscal-policy regimes; finds that longer "active" (stabilizing) regime durations lower the required steady-state debt-to-GDP ratio for sustainability — [Source](https://bundesbank.de/resource/blob/735820/2bac9821a2637689da1f62108398d66e/mL/2018-05-15-dkp-11-data.pdf) (abstract-only read; exact title/year not independently confirmed before session limit hit).
- Aldama, "Why fiscal regimes matter for fiscal sustainability analysis: an application to France," HAL/SciencesPo working paper. Applies a regime-switching sustainability test to French fiscal data; concludes regime *durations* matter as much as feedback coefficients for long-run sustainability assessment — [Source](https://hal-sciencespo.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03459336v1) (abstract-only read).
- Ferrari & Rodosthenous, Bielefeld CRC 1283 preprint. Models the debt-to-GDP ratio as a stochastic process whose drift (r−g) switches according to a continuous-time Markov chain; shows the optimal fiscal policy keeps the ratio within a band whose boundaries are regime-dependent — [Source](https://www.sfb1283.uni-bielefeld.de/preprints/sfb18039.pdf) (abstract-only read).
- Caldara, Cascaldi-Garcia, Cuba-Borda & Loria, "Understanding Growth-at-Risk" (conference paper hosted by the International Institute of Forecasters). Builds a Markov-switching model with *endogenous* transition probabilities that reproduces the empirical negative link between expected growth and growth volatility documented in the growth-at-risk literature, with financial-stress periods linked to both lower average future growth and downside-tilted risk — [Source](https://forecasters.org/wp-content/uploads/UnderstandingGAR.pdf) (abstract-only read; this is general GaR methodology, not debt-specific, but directly transferable).

### Inferences
- The field has not converged on explicit copulas as the dominant tool for joint macro-fiscal shock generation in *debt* DSA specifically; the one copula paper found (Zhang 2014) models cross-*country* spread dependence for crisis probability, not within-country joint macro-fiscal shocks feeding a debt-dynamics identity. This suggests copulas remain a less-trodden path in the debt-DSA literature proper, even though they are well established in sovereign-CDS/spread contagion modeling — a genuine technique gap the target model could flag as unexplored rather than assume is "already covered elsewhere."
- The ESM's "tilting" approach (BVAR draw, then reweight toward a judgmental/deterministic baseline) is a genuinely different design pattern from both (a) the target model's independent structural-parameter resampling and (b) a textbook VAR-shock approach — it is worth flagging to the target-model author as a named, operationally-used alternative for inducing correlated, state-informed, asymmetric uncertainty without a full copula or ML apparatus.
- Regime-switching (Markov-switching drift or fiscal-reaction-function regime) is a second distinct, published technique for capturing non-independent, state-dependent risk that is simpler to implement than a copula and arguably closer in spirit to the target model's existing "resample from named ranges" approach, since it just adds a discrete regime variable rather than requiring full joint-density estimation.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm full methodological detail of the Banque de France WP 1019 (how exactly correlated shocks/fat tails are handled, any explicit copula or t-distribution assumption) — the PDF text layer was inaccessible and the search/fetch budget was exhausted before a workaround (e.g., HTML abstract page) could be tried.
- No ML-based *joint scenario generation* (e.g., GAN- or variational-autoencoder-based macro scenario synthesis) specific to sovereign debt was found in the time available; this should be treated as "not found," not "doesn't exist" — a dedicated follow-up search on terms like "generative adversarial network macro scenario stress test sovereign" was planned but not executed before the session limit hit.

## Are there established methods for conditioning the stochastic distribution on the current state of the economy (genuine growth-at-risk/debt-at-risk quantile regression, not unconditional resampling) applied to an emerging-market or single-country context specifically?

### Takeaway
Outside the IMF's own cross-country panel "Debt-at-Risk" work (explicitly excluded from this scan), genuine single-country/EM conditional quantile applications specifically on *debt* are scarce in what surfaced; the closest material found is (a) a Mexico-specific growth-at-risk quantile-regression study conditioning growth risk on macro uncertainty (not fiscal/debt), and (b) the ESM's semi-conditional approach (BVAR + baseline-tilting + a probit early-warning model), which conditions a debt-risk *classification* on current fan-chart shape and country characteristics but is not a quantile regression in the Adrian-Boyarchenko-Giannone sense.

### Cited Findings
- Hitaj, Zavalloni & Zigraiova (2025), ESM Briefs 05/2025 (full text read). The ESM's framework conditions risk assessment on the *current* state via four fan-chart-derived metrics estimated for each country/quarter in real time: (1) probability of debt not stabilizing within 5 years, (2) fan-chart width (10th–90th percentile gap) at 5 years, (3) an interaction of distribution skewness and projected debt-to-GDP, and (4) an interaction of GDP size and (inverse) institutional quality. These four are combined via a **multivariate probit model** (760 country-quarter observations, euro-area sample from 2011) into a single crisis-probability signal, validated out of sample in a real-time-mimicking historical database — reported sensitivity (crisis detection) = 67%, specificity (non-crisis detection) = 81%, with thresholds calibrated so the missed-crisis rate stays ≤10% and the false-alarm rate stays ≤10% — [Source](https://www.esm.europa.eu/system/files/document/2025-11/ESM%20Brief%205%20-%20Debt%20sustainability%20under%20uncertainty.pdf).
- Zigraiova, D. & Erce, A. (2024), "Quantifying risks to sovereign market access," *IMF Economic Review*, pp. 1–63 — cited within the ESM brief as the source of the >350bp 10-year spread-over-Germany threshold used to define a "market-access distress" crisis episode for the ESM's validation exercise — [Source cited secondhand via the ESM brief above] (not independently fetched; full-text read not performed).
- "Growth at risk and macroeconomic uncertainty: Evidence from Mexico" (2025), *Estudios Económicos* (El Colegio de México). Estimates quantile regressions of annual output growth on lagged macroeconomic uncertainty, then fits the quantile-regression output to a skewed-t distribution of expected growth; finds that higher uncertainty significantly raises the probability of weak-growth outcomes — [Source](https://revistas.colmex.mx/index.php/Record/oai:oai.estudioseconomicos.colmex.mx:article-466) (abstract-only read; this is a growth-at-risk, not debt-at-risk, application, and is single-country/EM but not fiscal).
- C-GARP technical notes (CEMLA). Describes an open-source growth-at-risk toolkit explicitly built to cover both advanced and emerging economies using domestic and international macrofinancial variables; framed as adaptable rather than already fiscal/debt-specific — [Source](https://cgarp.cemla.org/C-GARP%20Technical%20Notes.pdf) (abstract-only read; not confirmed whether any published application uses it for debt-at-risk in a specific EM country).
- A 2026 arXiv paper on "Soft-Noncrossing Bayesian Panel Quantile Regression for Measuring Climate Tail Risk" reports country-level downside-growth-risk estimates for Brazil, India and Mexico among others (a one-standard-deviation climate shock's effect on the 5% expected shortfall of growth at horizon 1 is −0.54 for Brazil, −0.36 for India, −0.76 for Mexico) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.04664) (abstract/table-excerpt read only; this is climate-conditioned growth risk, not fiscal/debt risk, and methodology is a Bayesian — not classical — panel quantile approach, which is itself a technique variant worth noting for the "beyond simple VAR" question).

### Inferences
- The literature search did not surface a published, single-country (non-panel), genuine conditional-quantile debt-at-risk study for an emerging market outside the IMF's own (excluded) work. Where single-country conditional quantile work exists for EMs (Mexico), it targets growth, not debt or fiscal variables, leaving a plausible, citable gap: nobody found who has applied Adrian-Boyarchenko-Giannone-style conditional quantile regression to debt-to-GDP specifically for a single EM country such as Brazil.
- The ESM's probit-based conditioning (fan-chart metrics → crisis probability) is a meaningfully different conditioning architecture from quantile regression: it conditions a binary crisis-risk classification on moments of the *unconditional* stochastic distribution (width, skewness) plus country characteristics, rather than directly estimating conditional quantiles of the debt distribution itself. This is a usable, lower-complexity alternative the target model could adopt (fit a simple probit/logit to fan-chart-shape features against historical distress) without committing to full conditional-quantile machinery.

### Gaps
- No EM-specific or Brazil-specific debt-at-risk quantile regression paper was found; this may reflect true scarcity in the literature (consistent with the Key Questions framing, which already anticipated this) or may simply reflect the limited search budget (10 searches total across all five questions, cut short by a tool session limit before dedicated follow-up queries like "debt-at-risk Brazil quantile regression" or "sovereign risk quantile regression single country conditional" could be run).
- The Bayesian panel quantile regression technique (arXiv 2608.04664) is itself methodologically relevant to "conditioning + beyond-VAR joint distributions" but was only partially read (table excerpt); its applicability to fiscal/debt variables specifically is unconfirmed.

## Are there papers that validate a stochastic DSA model against real historical out-of-sample data (anchoring a projection at a past date and checking the actual subsequent path), and how do they handle validation failure?

### Takeaway
The clearest, most directly comparable validation exercise found is the ESM's own real-time historical back-test of its fan-chart framework (2011–present, 18 euro-area countries), which reports explicit accuracy metrics (sensitivity/specificity, missed-crisis and false-alarm rates) rather than treating validation as a pass/fail binary — but this validates whether fan-chart-derived *risk signals* predict crisis episodes, not strictly whether realized debt paths fall inside the fan at each horizon. A second, more modest example is a French fiscal-reaction-function paper using a genuine pseudo-out-of-sample vintage-data forecast comparison against the European Commission's own forecasts.

### Cited Findings
- Hitaj, Zavalloni & Zigraiova (2025), ESM Briefs 05/2025 (full text read). The ESM built "a historical database of fan charts starting from early 2011 for 18 euro area countries," with "each fan chart... produced by using only information available at the time, ensuring that the results mimic a real-time forecasting environment." Debt sustainability outcomes were judged using an objective market-stress threshold (10-year spread over Germany > 350bp, following Zigraiova & Erce 2024) rather than a subjective ex post judgment call. Reported performance: probability of a crisis following a low-risk signal = 2%; following a high-risk signal = 47%; sensitivity (correctly flagging true crises) = 67%; specificity (correctly flagging non-crises) = 81%; thresholds explicitly calibrated so the missed-crisis rate stays at or below 10% and the false-alarm rate stays below 10% — i.e., validation "failure" is handled not by discarding the model but by explicitly reporting and bounding the Type I/Type II error rates and recalibrating the two decision thresholds to keep both below a pre-set tolerance — [Source](https://www.esm.europa.eu/system/files/document/2025-11/ESM%20Brief%205%20-%20Debt%20sustainability%20under%20uncertainty.pdf).
- The same ESM brief cites "Bouabdallah et al., 2017, 'Debt sustainability analysis for euro area sovereigns: a methodological framework,' ECB Occasional Paper Series No. 185" as the methodological precursor/foundation — flagged here as a probable additional primary source on the underlying fan-chart construction, though not independently read — [Source cited secondhand] (not fetched).
- Dubbert (paper on time-varying fiscal reaction functions) evaluates the model in a genuine **pseudo-out-of-sample** exercise using real-time vintage datasets, comparing forecasts from a time-varying-coefficient model against a fixed-coefficient version and against the European Commission's own published forecasts, at multiple horizons — [Source: found via search synthesis, specific URL/venue not confirmed before session limit hit] (abstract-only, low-confidence citation — full bibliographic details unverified).
- Baret, Bec & Cochard (2025), Banque de France WP 1019. Reports only a weak consistency check — "the baseline lies comfortably within the middle of the distribution produced by our simulations" — which the paper itself appears to treat as reassurance rather than a formal backtest; no explicit discussion of what would constitute validation failure was found — [Source](https://www.banque-france.fr/system/files/2025-11/WP1019_0.pdf) (search-snippet read only).
- OBR Working Paper No. 17, "Evaluating forecast uncertainty with stochastic simulations." Notes that stochastic simulation can draw on a longer, more representative history of shocks than relying on historical forecast errors alone, and frames the method as widely used both academically and by other institutions including the IMF — [Source](https://obr.uk/download/working-paper-no-17-evaluating-forecast-uncertainty-with-stochastic-simulations) (abstract-only read; does not appear to contain a strict anchor-and-check backtest against realized debt paths based on what was retrievable).

### Inferences
- None of the sources found run the exact "anchor a projection at year T, check whether the realized debt-to-GDP path at T+5 falls within the stated percentile band" exercise that the target model's own back-test performs. The ESM brief is the closest analogue, but it validates a derived binary/probabilistic *risk signal* (crisis/no-crisis) against realized market-stress episodes, not the quantitative debt-path coverage rate of the fan chart itself. This is a methodologically meaningful difference worth flagging: the target model's back-test (anchoring at a past date and checking the actual subsequent debt path) is more directly a "probabilistic calibration / coverage" check, closer to what meteorologists call reliability diagrams, than what was found in this literature scan.
- Where validation is reported, institutions (ESM) do not treat a validation shortfall as invalidating the model; they instead convert it into calibrated error-rate disclosures (sensitivity/specificity, missed-crisis/false-alarm rates) and set decision thresholds accordingly. This is a transferable idea: rather than a pass/fail backtest verdict, report the back-test's empirical coverage rate (e.g., "the realized path fell inside the 50% band in X% of anchor-years") as a standing calibration diagnostic.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm the full citation (authors, year, journal) for the Dubbert fiscal-reaction-function paper — it surfaced only in a search-engine synthesis without a clean source URL, so it should be treated as a lead to re-verify, not a confirmed citation.
- No paper was found that explicitly discusses *what to do* when a stochastic DSA backtest fails outright (e.g., bands found to be systematically too narrow against realized history) beyond the ESM's implicit answer (recalibrate thresholds, report error rates). A dedicated search on "debt fan chart coverage rate backtest" or "probabilistic calibration public debt forecast" was planned but not completed before the session limit was reached.
- Could not verify whether the IMF's own fan-chart "candidate indicators" paper or the ECB Occasional Paper 185 (Bouabdallah et al. 2017) contains a more rigorous anchor-and-check backtest than the ESM brief describes — flagged as an unread but likely-relevant primary source.

## Any recent (2022–2026) machine-learning/deep-learning/ensemble-forecasting approaches to sovereign debt projection specifically, and how credible/adopted are they in the academic literature?

### Takeaway
Genuine ML/DL work that forecasts the *debt-to-GDP path itself* (as opposed to predicting a binary default/crisis event) is thin and recent: one peer-reviewed random-forest study on Italy (2024, *Journal of Economic Studies*) and one working paper combining a DSGE model with ML for France (2025) were found; a larger adjacent literature of ML-based sovereign *crisis/default/rating* classifiers exists but is dominated by theses, preprints, and practitioner-adjacent outlets rather than top field journals, suggesting academic adoption specifically for debt-path projection (vs. crisis classification) remains limited as of this scan.

### Cited Findings
- Sica, E., Altınbaş, H. & Marini, G.G. (2024), "Public debt forecasts and machine learning: the Italian case," *Journal of Economic Studies*, 51(6), pp. 1355–1370 (DOI: 10.1108/JES-07-2023-0337). Uses a random forest (ensemble ML) on Italian quarterly data, 2000–2021, to forecast debt-to-GDP; the abstract claims the method outperforms or avoids the limitations of DSGE models, stock-flow-consistent models, structural VARs, and neuro-fuzzy methods, but gives no quantitative error-metric comparison in the abstract itself — [Source](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JES-07-2023-0337/full/html) (fuller abstract-level read via fetch, but not the full paper body/results tables).
- BETA Working Paper #2025-18 (Bureau d'Économie Théorique et Appliquée, France). A hybrid framework pairing a DSGE model with machine learning to forecast France's public debt; the abstract frames ML as "underexplored in macro forecasting" because of short, low-frequency macro series — [Source](https://www.beta-economics.fr/working-papers/2025-18) (abstract-only read; whether the ML component is deep learning, gradient boosting, or another ensemble method was not confirmed).
- A 2024 Theseus (Finland) thesis on "Machine Learning for Small Data... Sovereign Debt Default Forecasting" tests imputation methods paired with ensemble models across 8,175 country-year observations; the best configuration (MICE imputation + LightGBM) reaches a test AUROC of 0.825 — [Source](https://theseus.fi/handle/10024/861416) (abstract-only read; this is a *default-prediction* classifier, not a debt-path projection model, and is a master's/bachelor's thesis, not peer-reviewed).
- A South African study compares fourteen ML techniques against logistic regression for predicting sovereign *credit ratings* using quarterly data 1999–2022, reporting that ML methods show superior precision — [Source](https://pure.uj.ac.za/en/publications/comparing-the-prediction-performance-of-machine-learning-algorith/) (abstract-only read; rating prediction, not debt-path projection).
- Alaminos et al. (2021), published in *Symmetry*, compares computational techniques (fuzzy decision trees, AdaBoost, XGBoost) against statistical methods for predicting sovereign debt and currency crises, finding the computational techniques more precise — [Source: found via search synthesis] (abstract-only, pre-dates the 2022–2026 window specified in the assignment, included only as adjacent context, not as a direct answer).
- A 2025 preprint (later stated to be published in *FinTech*) applies supervised ML to 20 EU member states, 2000–2024, to predict fiscal-stress episodes, arguing conventional DSA frameworks rely on "static thresholds and linear dynamics" that the authors view as inadequate — [Source](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202504.2189/v1) (abstract-only read; a preprint platform, not yet confirmed peer-reviewed at the time of this scan despite the claimed later FinTech publication).
- SUERF Policy Brief, "Can machine learning help forecasting fiscal crises in emerging and developing countries?" Reports that ML methods can outperform standard econometric approaches on accuracy for forecasting fiscal crises — [Source](https://www.suerf.org/suerf-policy-brief/66447/can-machine-learning-help-forecasting-fiscal-crises-in-emerging-and-developing-countries) (a policy-brief/think-tank outlet, not a peer-reviewed journal — explicitly noted as practitioner-adjacent per the assignment's own framing).

### Inferences
- There is a clear split in this literature: (a) a larger, faster-growing body of ML work on *binary* fiscal/debt/currency crisis classification (ratings, defaults, stress episodes), published across theses, preprints, and policy briefs with only a few peer-reviewed journal entries (Symmetry, SUERF-adjacent, FinTech); versus (b) a much smaller body that forecasts the *continuous debt-to-GDP path* itself, where only the Sica, Altınbaş & Marini (2024) *Journal of Economic Studies* piece and the BETA working paper were found. This suggests that if the target model wanted to cite credible academic (not blog) precedent for an ML-based forecasting extension, the debt-path random-forest paper (Italy) is the most directly comparable and most clearly peer-reviewed example found, while the crisis-classification literature, though larger, answers a different question (will a crisis happen) than the target model's task (what is the distribution of the debt path).
- None of the sources found use deep learning (neural networks, transformers) with demonstrated, published, peer-reviewed results specifically for sovereign debt-path projection within 2022–2026; the one deep-learning-named effort (Slovenia, RNN/LSTM/Transformer) is a project description with no results yet, and a 2020 arXiv deep-learning paper on *bank* balance-sheet stress testing (DeepStress) is outside both the date window and the sovereign-debt topic. This supports treating deep learning for debt-path projection as still largely unestablished in the academic literature as of this scan, consistent with the kind of cautious framing the target model already uses for its own GaR/DaR extension.

## Are there papers explicitly modeling debt composition (currency, indexation, maturity) jointly with stochastic simulation, beyond a simple revaluation exercise?

### Takeaway
Yes — three distinct, concrete examples were found, spanning an academic/preprint theoretical model, a debt-management-office (UK) simulation engine, and a Brazilian National Treasury survey paper, all going beyond simple mark-to-market revaluation by jointly simulating issuance/maturity choices, yield-curve dynamics (including inflation-linked indexation), and correlated interest-rate/deficit shocks.

### Cited Findings
- Cameron (U.S. Treasury-affiliated author, per search synthesis), "Long-Run Sovereign Debt Composition: An Analytic Ergodic Framework with Explicit Maturity Structure," arXiv:2602.19892 (dated February 2026). Builds a discrete-time model of sovereign debt tracking maturity explicitly via a fixed issuance allocation across a maturity ladder; derives closed-form steady-state portfolio shares for the deterministic case, and in the stochastic extension shows Monte Carlo debt simulations converge to the system's long-run averages regardless of starting conditions (given a long enough simulation horizon); finds that correlation between interest-rate shocks and deficit shocks adds explicit correction terms relative to the deterministic solution — [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19892v1) (abstract/results-summary read via search synthesis, not the full paper; author's full name and institutional affiliation not independently confirmed — treat the attribution to "U.S. Treasury" cautiously pending direct verification).
- Pick & Anthony, UK Debt Management Office, "Simulation model for analysing the composition of UK government bond issuance" (DMO working paper). Combines a macroeconomic model, separate Nelson-Siegel yield-curve models for conventional and inflation-linked (i.e., indexed) gilts, and a debt-issuance engine; running alternative issuance strategies through the model produces full distributions of debt-servicing cost that can be compared on a cost/risk (mean-variance-style) basis across strategies — directly relevant to joint currency/indexation/maturity modeling since it treats the inflation-linked and conventional yield curves as separate, jointly-simulated stochastic processes rather than a single revaluation shock — [Source](https://www.dmo.gov.uk/media/kuzlojeq/sda160806.pdf) (abstract-only read).
- Proite, A., Brazilian National Treasury / ENAP, "Texto para discussão n. 18: sovereign debt risk modeling and portfolio management" (ENAP working paper series). Surveys the family of models used by sovereign debt management offices for portfolio composition, ranging from balance-sheet/net-worth optimization to deterministic and stochastic debt-simulation approaches; derives Value-at-Risk-style risk measures to assess alternative funding/composition strategies; concludes practitioners favor simulation models over balance-sheet optimization — [Source](https://repositorio.enap.gov.br/bitstream/1/4468/1/2015%20TEXTOS_Texto%2018%20%28ingl%c3%aas%29.pdf) (abstract-only read; notably this is a Brazilian-institution source, directly relevant to the target model's country context, though it is a survey/methodology paper rather than a novel empirical stochastic DSA application).

### Inferences
- All three sources treat debt composition (maturity structure, and in the UK case indexation) as a *choice variable* feeding into a stochastic simulation of financing cost/risk, which is a materially richer treatment than a one-off currency/indexation revaluation shock bolted onto an otherwise single-instrument debt stock — this directly answers the question and suggests a concrete extension path for the target model: instead of (or in addition to) its existing "debt composition FX" stochastic noise block, it could adopt a DMO-style issuance-strategy simulation (as in the UK DMO paper) that jointly simulates the conventional and indexed/FX-linked yield curves rather than applying a single aggregated shock.
- The Proite ENAP paper is particularly notable for being a Brazilian National Treasury publication surveying exactly this modeling family; it is a strong, directly-relevant, diverse (non-IMF, non-academic-journal, practitioner/government) source that the target model's author may want to read in full given the country-specific relevance, even though it was only abstract-read here.

### Gaps
- None of the three sources were read beyond the abstract/summary level; in particular, it is unconfirmed whether the Cameron arXiv paper's "correlation between interest-rate and deficit shocks" treatment amounts to a full joint density (copula-like) or a simpler linear-correlation adjustment — this distinction matters for comparison against the target model's existing bootstrapped stock-flow-adjustment noise term and was not resolved before the session limit was reached.
- The Cameron paper's author affiliation and exact publication status (arXiv preprint only, or also submitted to a journal) could not be verified; treat the "U.S. Treasury" attribution as unconfirmed.
