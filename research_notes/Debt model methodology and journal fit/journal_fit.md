## Key Question 1: Does Fiscal Studies regularly publish single-country, simulation/microsimulation-heavy, debt-sustainability-adjacent papers of this type and length?

### Takeaway
No clear evidence that it does. Repeated targeted searches turned up exactly one debt-sustainability-adjacent article in Fiscal Studies in the 2020–2026 window, and it is a conceptual/accounting ("balance-sheet") framework paper, not a stochastic Monte Carlo microsimulation model — it does not match the paper's methodology (1,000-draw Monte Carlo, household tax-benefit microsimulation, multi-block macro-micro linkage) or its single-country emerging-market-application framing. No microsimulation-plus-debt-sustainability paper on Brazil, or any other single emerging-market country, was found in Fiscal Studies.

### Cited Findings
- Fiscal Studies is a quarterly, peer-reviewed journal published by Wiley-Blackwell for the Institute for Fiscal Studies (IFS), founded 1979, aiming to "bridge the gap between academic research and policy" via applied microeconomics on how policy affects households, firms and government finances; example topics listed include public finances since the recession, higher-education finance, corporate taxation, labour supply, poverty and inequality, wealth, and well-being/productivity measurement — none of these example topics explicitly names debt sustainability or microsimulation-based DSA — [Fiscal Studies (Wikipedia) and aggregator scope summaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_Studies)
- The one identified debt-sustainability-adjacent article: Eduardo Levy Yeyati and Federico Sturzenegger, "A Balance-Sheet Approach to Fiscal Sustainability," Fiscal Studies, vol. 44(1), pp. 61–84, March 2023 (per IDEAS/RePEc listing) — [RePEc/IDEAS listing](https://ideas.repec.org/p/sad/wpaper/150.html), working-paper versions at [HKS/CID Working Paper 150](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cid/publications/faculty-working-papers/cid-working-paper-no.-150) and [Universidad de San Andrés / RedNIE 2021-55](https://rednie.eco.unc.edu.ar/files/DT/2021-55.pdf)
- That paper's method is explicitly non-stochastic and non-microsimulation: it argues debt sustainability should be judged on a full government balance sheet (present discounted value of future taxes as the main asset, present discounted value of expenditures plus explicit debt as the main liability), not solely on explicit debt levels, and shows how net worth responds to growth, commodity-price, or real-exchange-rate shocks deterministically/analytically — [working paper abstract via HKS/CID](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cid/publications/faculty-working-papers/cid-working-paper-no.-150)
- Searches explicitly combining "Fiscal Studies" with "microsimulation," "Brazil," and "emerging market" returned no matching journal article; all Brazil-focused tax-benefit microsimulation work found (e.g., the BRAHMS model) appeared as IZA/World Bank working papers, not in Fiscal Studies — [IZA DP 2114](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp2114/1/citations/ris), [World Bank Brazil microsimulation feasibility paper](https://microdata.worldbank.org/citations/2601)
- The literature on stochastic/Monte Carlo debt sustainability analysis for emerging markets (Karam & Hostland 2005/2006 World Bank/IMF working papers; Tanner & Samaké 2008; Garcia & Rigobon 2004 NBER; Navarro-Ortiz & Sapena 2020) does not include any Fiscal Studies entries among the papers surfaced — see Key Question 2 findings below for full citations.

### Inferences
- Fiscal Studies' demonstrated comparative advantage is applied UK/OECD tax-benefit microeconomics and policy evaluation (its parent institute IFS runs the UK's leading tax-benefit microsimulation model, TAXBEN), not stochastic macro-fiscal debt-sustainability modelling with Monte Carlo propagation — the one DSA-adjacent piece found is a conceptual accounting reframing by two Latin American macroeconomists, suggesting the journal is open to debt-sustainability topics in principle, but evidently only rarely, and not in the stochastic-simulation genre.
- The length and number of model blocks described (9 blocks, ~44 pages, extensive appendices) is well outside what a single Fiscal Studies article is likely to accommodate; Fiscal Studies articles are generally shorter, single-method applied-micro papers (inference; could not confirm exact typical length from retrieved sources since the Wiley page itself was inaccessible).

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the actual 2023–2026 Fiscal Studies table of contents from Wiley directly (403 error), so this finding rests on targeted search rather than a full issue-by-issue scan. It is possible additional debt-sustainability or microsimulation pieces exist that these searches did not surface.
- Could not confirm Fiscal Studies' typical article length/page-count distribution from a primary source.

## Key Question 2: Most plausible alternative journals — fit characterization

### Takeaway
The clearest, most directly evidenced fit is the IMF Economic Review lineage (via its predecessor IMF Staff Papers, which published a near-identical stochastic/Monte Carlo DSA study of Brazil, Mexico, and Turkey). Economic Modelling has a directly confirmed track record publishing probabilistic/Monte Carlo external-debt-sustainability work (cross-country). International Tax and Public Finance and Journal of Policy Modeling are both plausible by stated scope (single-country public finance; policy-relevant empirical modelling) but no directly matching published example was found for either. National Tax Journal is primarily a US-focused venue and a weaker fit for a Brazil case study. Brazilian/Latin-American domestic journals (Estudos Econômicos, and by extension EconomiA/Revista Brasileira de Economia) are an active, confirmed home for Brazil-specific stochastic debt-sustainability work, supporting the paper's stated secondary audience of Brazilian policy readers.

### Cited Findings
- **IMF Economic Review** (successor to IMF Staff Papers, launched 2010, published by Palgrave/Springer Nature for the IMF): states its mission as "dedicated to publishing peer-reviewed, high-quality, context-related academic research on open-economy macroeconomics," explicitly welcoming work that "borrows from, and interacts with, other fields such as finance, international trade, political economy, labor, economic history or development," aiming to reach policymakers as well as academics — [IMF Economic Review / Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMF_Economic_Review); succession announced by the IMF in 2009 — [IMF 2009 announcement](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/survey/so/2009/bok100509a.htm)
- Its predecessor journal, **IMF Staff Papers**, published exactly the kind of study this paper resembles: Tanner & Samaké, "Probabilistic Sustainability of Public Debt: A Vector Autoregression Approach for Brazil, Mexico, and Turkey," IMF Staff Papers, vol. 55 (2008), pp. 149–182, DOI 10.1057/palgrave.imfsp.9450029 — a Monte Carlo/VAR-based stochastic DSA study explicitly covering Brazil as one of three single-country case applications — [IDEAS/RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/imfstp/v55y2008i1p149-182.html), [IMF working-paper version](https://imf.org/external/pubs/ft/staffp/2008/01/pdf/tanner.pdf)
- **Economic Modelling** (Elsevier) has a confirmed, directly on-topic example: Navarro-Ortiz & Sapena, "Is external debt sustainable? A probabilistic approach," Economic Modelling, vol. 93 (2020), pp. 142–153 — estimates a VAR on IMF data across 38 countries, then runs Monte Carlo simulations to build each country's probability distribution of debt repayment capacity — [RePEc/ScienceDirect listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/eeeecmode/v_3a93_3ay_3a2020_3ai_3ac_3ap_3a142-153.htm)
- **Journal of Policy Modeling** (Elsevier, for the Society for Policy Modeling): stated scope is a forum for "analysis of international policy issues" with emphasis on economic/social/political interdependencies, favoring formal modelling validated on replicable data, and explicitly asking whether submissions "yield lessons for policymakers" while discouraging purely technique-focused submissions — [Elsevier journal page](https://shop.elsevier.com/journals/journal-of-policy-modeling/0161-8938). A confirmed developing-country-debt example exists in the journal (Siddique, Selvanathan & Selvanathan on external debt and growth in highly indebted poor countries, vol. 38, issue 5, 2016), though this is a growth/debt-burden study, not a stochastic DSA or microsimulation paper — found via [IDEAS/RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/repec:eee:jpolmo).
- **International Tax and Public Finance** (Springer, for the International Institute of Public Finance): stated scope covers "original research, both theoretical and empirical, in all areas of public economics," broadly interpreted to include expenditure and financing policies, with "special emphasis on open economy or, more generally, interjurisdictional issues," but explicitly states single-country research (e.g., single-country tax reform analysis) is also welcome, and it carries a "Policy Watch" section for current policy issues — [Editage/journal profile summary](https://www.editage.com/research-solutions/journal/international-tax-and-public-finance/15245). No directly matching stochastic-DSA-plus-microsimulation example was found in searches; this is a scope-based plausibility assessment, not a confirmed precedent.
- **National Tax Journal** (University of Chicago Press, for the National Tax Association): its own "About" page states a 2024 JCR impact factor of 1.3, ranked #332 of 617 Economics journals — [NTJ About page summary via search](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/journals/ntj/about). The journal is historically and substantively US-tax-policy-centered (inference based on general knowledge of the journal and its sponsoring association; the scope statement itself could not be directly retrieved due to a 404 error). This makes it a comparatively weak fit for a Brazil-specific application, notwithstanding its respectable standing within US public-finance circles.
- **Brazilian/Latin-American domestic outlets actively publish this exact genre on Brazil specifically**: Luporini, "Debt Sustainability Under Uncertainty: a tentative simulation model for Brazil," IE-UFRJ Discussion Paper 004/2026, uses a Markov-switching Monte Carlo debt model — [UFRJ discussion paper](https://www.ie.ufrj.br/images/IE/TDS/2026/TD_IE_004_2026_LUPORINI.pdf); Couri (2016, UnB master's thesis) runs stochastic/Monte Carlo projections of Brazilian gross debt — [UnB repository](https://repositorio.unb.br/bitstream/10482/22811/1/2016_DanielVelosoCouri.pdf); Luporini's related fiscal-reaction-function work on Brazil was published in Estudos Econômicos (USP) — [USP journal listing](https://revistas.usp.br/ee/article/view/56353). No confirmed example specifically in EconomiA or Revista Brasileira de Economia was found, but the pattern shows Brazilian academic outlets are an active, receptive venue for this material, consistent with the paper's stated secondary audience of Brazilian policy readers.
- No matching examples were found in this research for Journal of Public Economics, Journal of Macroeconomics, Review of Development Economics, Emerging Markets Review, or World Bank Economic Review specifically combining stochastic DSA + microsimulation + single-country emerging-market application — see Gaps.

### Inferences
- The paper's specific combination — stochastic/Monte Carlo debt-sustainability analysis as the macro spine, with a household microsimulation module bolted on for distributional/poverty outcomes, applied to one emerging-market country — does not have an obvious single "home" journal; it straddles development macroeconomics (IMF Economic Review-type venues), applied policy modelling (Journal of Policy Modeling, Economic Modelling), and public finance/microsimulation (International Tax and Public Finance), without being a strong central match for any one of them, and is arguably a weaker match for Fiscal Studies than for several of these alternatives given the complete absence of a comparable precedent there.
- Given the paper's explicit application to a live 2026 Brazilian presidential election and its stated secondary audience of Brazilian policy readers, a Brazilian or Latin-American-focused outlet (EconomiA, Revista Brasileira de Economia, or even a policy-oriented venue like a Brazilian-focused special issue) deserves serious consideration as a complementary (not necessarily primary) target, alongside an English-language, internationally indexed journal for the academic audience.

### Gaps
- No confirmed published precedent was found in Journal of Public Economics, Journal of Macroeconomics, Review of Development Economics, Emerging Markets Review, World Bank Economic Review, or EconomiA/Revista Brasileira de Economia specifically matching this paper's stochastic-DSA + microsimulation + single-country combination; the WebSearch session limit interrupted direct scope-page verification for several of these before they could be checked as thoroughly as the others.
- Could not retrieve Elsevier's own aims-and-scope statement for Economic Modelling or Journal of Macroeconomics directly (403 errors), so their "both stochastic DSA and microsimulation" fit rests on the one Economic Modelling precedent found plus general journal reputation, not a verified scope statement.

## Key Question 3: Prestige/standing of Fiscal Studies vs. alternatives

### Takeaway
Available metrics are inconsistent across sources and should be treated cautiously, but the overall picture is that Fiscal Studies is a solidly well-regarded, policy-oriented journal (SJR ~2.1–2.3, reported Q1 in Economics/Econometrics by at least one SCImago-based aggregator) whose Clarivate Journal Impact Factor data shows a suspicious and likely non-representative spike to 7.3–7.4 in 2022 before falling back to roughly 1.1–1.3 in 2024–2025 — a pattern consistent with a small-journal citation-count anomaly rather than a durable prestige shift. IMF Economic Review appears to sit at a comparable or higher tier (SJR ~2.08, Q1, h-index 85 per one aggregator) but with equally unverified primary-source figures. National Tax Journal is a notch below on raw impact factor (1.3) despite a reported SCImago Q1. Economic Modelling and Journal of Policy Modeling could not be benchmarked with retrieved metrics in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- Fiscal Studies: Wikipedia cites a Journal Citation Reports 2022 impact factor of 7.3 — [Fiscal Studies (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_Studies). SciJournal's table (sourced to JCR) shows a volatile series: 2020 = 0.896, 2021 = 5.867, 2022 = 7.439, 2024 ≈ 1.160, 2025 ≈ 1.300 — [SciJournal Fiscal Studies impact-factor page](https://www.scijournal.org/impact-factor-of-fiscal-studies.shtml). SCImago-sourced SJR figures from the same aggregator: 2022 SJR = 2.103, 2023 SJR = 2.267 — [SciJournal](https://scijournal.org/fisc-stud). A separate aggregator (ResearchBite, Scopus-based) reports Fiscal Studies' best quartile as Q1 — [ResearchBite](https://www.researchbite.com/impact/details/27094). A third aggregator (Journal Metrics, OpenAlex-based) gives conflicting internal results — Q2 for both "Economics and Econometrics" and "Accounting" categories alongside an overall "Q1" label on the same page — [Journal Metrics Fiscal Studies page](https://www.journalmetrics.org/journal/fiscal-studies). No cause for the 2021→2022 impact-factor spike (5.867 → 7.439, then collapsing to ~1.2 by 2024) was found in any retrieved source; this is treated as an unexplained anomaly, with the 2024–2025 figures (~1.1–1.3) likely a better read of the journal's durable standing than the 2022 peak.
- IMF Economic Review: a Resurchify listing reports an SJR of 2.079, h-index of 85, Q1 best quartile, and a 2024 "impact score" of 3.13 (reported as a decrease from the prior year) — [Resurchify, cited via search summary]. A separate aggregator (Journalsinsights) gives a conflicting 2026 impact factor of 4.3, which could not be corroborated and should not be relied on — flagged as unverified.
- World Bank Economic Review: Oxford University Press's own About page reportedly states a 2025 impact factor of 2.3 and 5-year impact factor of 3.8, with category ranks of Economics 210/626, Development Studies 25/66, Business/Finance 102/243; a Scopus CiteScore of 5.1 for 2025 — [OUP WBER About page, via search summary](https://academic.oup.com/wber/pages/About). A conflicting Journal Metrics figure gives a 2025 impact factor of 1.8 and JCR Q2. These are flagged as conflicting and not independently verified against the primary OUP or JCR portal in this research pass.
- National Tax Journal: its own About page (University of Chicago Press) reportedly states a 2024 JCR impact factor of 1.3, ranked #332 of 617 Economics journals, and a 2024 CiteScore of 2.8; a Resurchify/SCImago-sourced listing separately reports an SJR of 1.437, h-index of 70, and best quartile Q1 across Accounting, Economics and Econometrics, and Finance categories — illustrating that SCImago (SJR, citation-network-based) and Clarivate JCR (raw citation-count impact factor) can rank the same journal very differently.
- No ABS Academic Journal Guide (2024) star ratings could be retrieved for any of the candidate journals (registration-gated database; search did not surface specific entries) — this is a genuine gap given the task's suggested use of ABS/ABDC rankings.

### Inferences
- On a Scopus/SCImago (SJR, Q1-ish) basis, Fiscal Studies, National Tax Journal, and IMF Economic Review all cluster in a broadly comparable "respectable field journal" tier; none of the three looks dramatically more prestigious than the others once the Fiscal Studies 2022 JCR spike is discounted as an outlier. World Bank Economic Review appears to be a tier above on most metrics (higher CiteScore, broader Economics-category reach), consistent with its status as a flagship multilateral-institution research journal.
- Given this rough parity in prestige among Fiscal Studies, National Tax Journal, and IMF Economic Review, journal choice should be driven primarily by topical/methodological fit (Key Questions 1–2) rather than by chasing a marginally higher impact factor — none of the differences found here are large enough to be decisive on their own, and the metrics themselves are too inconsistent across sources to trust at more than one decimal place.

### Gaps
- Could not access scimagojr.com or Clarivate Journal Citation Reports directly; all figures above are second-hand via aggregator sites of varying reliability, several of which contradicted each other on the same journal.
- No ABS/ABDC rating retrieved for any candidate journal.
- Economic Modelling and Journal of Policy Modeling were not benchmarked on any impact/quartile metric in this research pass due to time/tool constraints.

## Key Question 4: Fiscal Studies' stance on papers declining to take a normative/ranking position on a live political choice

### Takeaway
No explicit editorial policy or stated precedent was found, in either direction, on whether Fiscal Studies journal articles analyze live political/electoral fiscal programmes while declining to rank them. What was found instead is a clear pattern at the level of the parent institute (IFS, not the academic journal): IFS routinely publishes evaluative, non-journal "Observations" and election briefings that assess the fiscal credibility of UK party manifestos (sometimes explicitly judging them "not credible" or lacking "realism"), but this evaluative political commentary appears to run through IFS's general publications/press activity, not through peer-reviewed Fiscal Studies articles. This is a meaningful distinction the authors should weigh: the parent institute is comfortable being evaluative about live politics, but there is no evidence the journal itself has published an academic article that analyzes two live, named electoral fiscal programmes (ranked or unranked) in the way this paper does.

### Cited Findings
- IFS (publisher) assessed the UK Conservative and Labour 2024 manifestos and reportedly said they "largely ignored" the facts about the public finances and failed to be honest with voters, per IFS director Paul Johnson — [FullFact summary of IFS 2024 analysis](https://fullfact.org/politics/ifs-conservative-labour-manifestos/)
- IFS's 2019 election analysis found neither major UK party's manifesto spending pledges "credible" — [reporting on IFS 2019 analysis, via search summary]
- IFS's 2021 analysis found the SNP, Scottish Labour and Scottish Conservative manifestos shared "a lack of realism" about funding their commitments — [Scotsman coverage of IFS 2021 analysis](https://www.scotsman.com/news/politics/scottish-election-2021-snp-scottish-labour-and-scottish-conservatives-manifestos-are-disconnected-from-fiscal-reality-says-ifs-3214129)
- These IFS manifesto-costing assessments are institutional/press publications (IFS "Observations," briefings, and media commentary), not identified as peer-reviewed Fiscal Studies journal articles — this is an inference from the sourcing (all citations found are news coverage or IFS communications, not journal citations), not a confirmed negative (i.e., it remains possible that some manifesto-costing analysis has also appeared in the journal itself, but none was surfaced).
- A directly relevant academic article on UK manifesto costing politics was found, but it is not in Fiscal Studies: "'Where's the money coming from?' Manifesto costings and the politics of fiscal credibility in UK general elections, 1955–2019," published in the British Journal of Politics and International Relations (SAGE), which argues forward-looking fiscal debate during elections tends to favor incumbents — [search summary of BJPIR article].
- No search surfaced any explicit Fiscal Studies author-guidelines statement on political neutrality, normative advocacy, or ranking of policy alternatives.

### Inferences
- Fiscal Studies' institutional parent (IFS) is demonstrably willing to be publicly evaluative — even critical — about live party manifestos, which suggests an IFS-adjacent readership would not be allergic to policy-relevant, politically-timed material in principle. However, since this evaluative activity is channeled through IFS's non-journal publications rather than through Fiscal Studies itself, it is not strong evidence that the journal's peer-review process or editorial board specifically welcomes (or has ever handled) a paper analyzing two live, named candidates' programmes — ranked or not. This is genuinely a gap rather than a basis for confidence either way.
- The paper's choice to decline ranking the two programmes is, if anything, more cautious than IFS's own public track record (which does rank/criticize manifestos), so it is unlikely to be seen as a liability by Fiscal Studies' editorial culture even absent a direct precedent — but this is a reasoned inference, not a cited fact.

### Gaps
- No Fiscal Studies author guidelines or editorial-policy page was retrieved (Wiley page returned 403 on every attempt), so no official statement on this question could be checked.
- No confirmed example of a Fiscal Studies journal article (as opposed to an IFS institutional publication) analyzing a live electoral fiscal programme was found, in either direction (i.e., no example of the journal doing this exists in the collected evidence — but a full issue-by-issue 2020–2026 search was not completed, so this is not a confident negative).

## Key Question 5: Overall recommendation

### Takeaway
Based on the evidence gathered, Fiscal Studies should be treated as a plausible secondary/backup target rather than a confident first choice: it has essentially no track record publishing this paper's specific genre (stochastic Monte Carlo debt-sustainability modelling combined with household microsimulation, applied to a single emerging-market country), notwithstanding one adjacent conceptual (non-stochastic) debt-sustainability piece. The strongest, most directly evidenced first-choice alternative is a venue in the IMF Economic Review lineage, given its predecessor (IMF Staff Papers) published a near-identical stochastic/Monte Carlo DSA study of Brazil specifically, its stated scope explicitly invites open-economy macro work connecting to development/political economy, and it targets both academic and policy audiences. Economic Modelling is the strongest second alternative given its confirmed, specific precedent of publishing a Monte Carlo probabilistic external-debt-sustainability paper.

### Inferences
- First-choice recommendation: target the IMF Economic Review (or, failing fit/acceptance there, directly cite and position the paper relative to the IMF Staff Papers / Tanner & Samaké (2008) lineage when approaching comparable outlets), because it is the only venue found with a essentially identical prior publication — a Monte Carlo/VAR stochastic DSA study naming Brazil as one of its single-country applications — and because its stated mission (open-economy macro research relevant to both academics and policymakers) matches this paper's dual academic/policy-reader aim better than Fiscal Studies' UK-centric applied-microeconomics identity.
- Second-choice/parallel recommendation: Economic Modelling, given its confirmed 2020 precedent for probabilistic/Monte Carlo external-debt-sustainability analysis, broader "applied modelling" identity that can accommodate the paper's many model blocks and appendices, and general field reputation as a receptive venue for quantitative macro-development work; it is less policy-audience-focused than IMF Economic Review but likely more accommodating of the sheer length and multi-block structure described.
- A plausible complementary (not substitute) placement, given the paper's explicit secondary aim of reaching Brazilian policy readers and its application to a live 2026 Brazilian election: a Brazilian domestic outlet (e.g., EconomiA, Revista Brasileira de Economia, or Estudos Econômicos) for a companion piece or Portuguese-language version, since this is where confirmed precedent for Brazil-specific stochastic debt work already exists.
- Fiscal Studies should not be ruled out entirely — it is a reputable, policy-engaged journal and the Levy Yeyati & Sturzenegger (2023) precedent shows the editorial board is willing to publish debt-sustainability-themed work from outside the UK — but the authors should go in with realistic expectations: there is no evidence the journal has ever handled a paper of this methodological type (1,000-draw Monte Carlo, 9 model blocks, household microsimulation, honest back-test failure, non-ranked dual-candidate election application) or length (~44 pages), and reviewers drawn from its typical UK-applied-microeconomics / public-finance pool may not be the best-matched referees for a stochastic macro-fiscal simulation model. Treat it as a legitimate secondary/backup option, not the first-choice submission.

### Gaps
- This recommendation is based on topical/methodological precedent-matching and partial, inconsistent prestige metrics; it does not account for practical factors outside this research's scope (e.g., current editor relationships, typical turnaround times, open-access fee policies, or the authors' own prior publication history/network), which could reasonably shift the practical first choice among the two or three journals identified as strongest fits.
- Could not fully verify ABS/ABDC rankings for any journal, which the task specifically suggested as a source; this should be checked directly against the paywalled Chartered ABS portal before finalizing a submission strategy.
