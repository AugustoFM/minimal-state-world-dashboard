# Recent (2022-2026) Developments in Sovereign Debt-Sustainability Forecasting and Fiscal Risk Modelling Beyond Classical Stochastic/VAR DSA

Scope note: this survey excludes growth-at-risk/debt-at-risk quantile regression (Prasad et al. 2019 IMF; Lian, Presbitero & Wiriadinata 2020; Furceri et al. 2025), since the target model already knows that literature. It also treats "classical stochastic/VAR-based DSA" (IMF/EC fan charts built from historical-shock bootstraps or simple VARs) as the baseline to be improved upon, not as new.

## What machine-learning or deep-learning approaches have been applied to sovereign debt / fiscal-risk forecasting, and how do they perform against traditional econometric benchmarks?

### Takeaway
There is a real and growing 2021-2024 literature applying tree-ensemble ML (random forest, XGBoost/LightGBM, gradient boosting) and some deep learning to sovereign-crisis and debt-distress *classification/early-warning* problems, with several papers (mostly working papers / policy briefs rather than top journals) reporting that ML beats logit/probit benchmarks on accuracy or AUC — but the strongest counter-finding, from a 2026-dated World Bank paper, is that a simple probit model actually predicts debt distress better than more sophisticated algorithms. No source found applies ML to full stochastic *debt-path* fan-chart projection (i.e., replacing the Monte Carlo propagation itself); the ML work is almost entirely early-warning/binary-crisis classification, not continuous debt-trajectory forecasting. Treat accuracy gains as domain- and sample-specific, not an established general result.

### Cited Findings
- De Marchi & Moro (Bank of Italy, Temi di discussione / working paper no. 1405, 2023; published as "Forecasting Fiscal Crises in Emerging Markets and Low-Income Countries with Machine Learning Models," *Open Economies Review*, vol. 35(1), pp. 189-213, Feb. 2024) report that machine-learning techniques outperform standard econometric approaches, specifically the probit model, for forecasting fiscal crises in emerging/developing economies; the abstract gives no specific AUC/accuracy numbers and the full-text PDF was not retrieved (abstract-only reading) — [Bank of Italy working paper / RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/p/bdi/wptemi/td_1405_23.html); [journal version](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/openec/v35y2024i1d10.1007_s11079-023-09722-9.html); summarized also in a SUERF policy brief, [SUERF 66447](https://www.suerf.org/suerf-policy-brief/66447/can-machine-learning-help-forecasting-fiscal-crises-in-emerging-and-developing-countries).
- A World Bank working paper (RePEc entry wbk/wbrwps/11441, dated 2026 in the metadata — date unverified/possibly a repository artifact) finds the opposite result for debt distress specifically: simple single-equation probit models have better predictive power for debt distress than more sophisticated ML algorithms — found only via secondary search snippet, not fetched in full text — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:11441). This is a direct, citable counter-example to ML-hype claims and should be reported alongside the positive results.
- IMF WP/20/001 "Debt Is Not Free" (2020) and a related Hellwig (2021) IMF WP 2021/150 "Predicting Fiscal Crises: A Machine Learning Approach" both predate the 2022 cutoff but establish the IMF's own prior ML-for-fiscal-crisis baseline (public debt as leading indicator, strong non-linearities) — abstract/secondary citation only, not fetched — [IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2020/01/03/debt-is-not-free-48894).
- IMF WP/24/54 (March 2024), Batsuuri, He, Hu, Leslie & Lutz, "Predicting IMF-Supported Programs: A Machine Learning Approach" — applies ML to forecast which countries enter IMF-supported programs (a proxy for debt/fiscal distress), not debt sustainability paths directly — abstract-level only — [IMF](https://imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WP/2024/English/wpiea2024054-print-pdf.ashx).
- A 2024 *Cogent Economics & Finance* article, "Public external debt sustainability assessment: towards a machine learning based approach" (vol. 12, issue 1) exists but is paywalled; found via title/citation only, content unverified — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/oaefxx/v12y2024i1p2429770.html).
- Rafie & Lekhal (2024) combine a cointegration test with ML classifiers and explainable-AI (feature-importance) methods on 22 middle-income countries, 2000-2021, finding that each predictor's effect on debt sustainability is threshold-dependent and that exchange-rate dynamics are a critical stress channel — secondary search-summary only, title/venue not independently confirmed — found via aggregator snippet.
- A 2025 study (published in *FinTech*, vol. 4, issue 3, 2025) applies supervised ML classifiers, including XGBoost, to macro-fiscal panel data for 20 EU member states (2000-2024) and finds XGBoost gives the highest predictive accuracy for fiscal-stress classification but shows signs of overfitting — a candid, non-hyped result — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jfinte/v4y2025i3p27-d1686977.html); abstract/secondary-summary level.
- A 2024 Finnish master's thesis (Theseus repository) tests multiple-imputation + ensemble ML (LightGBM) on 8,175 country-year observations (180 countries, 1970-2019) for sovereign default forecasting, reporting a test AUROC of 0.825 with MICE-imputed + LightGBM — this is a thesis, not peer-reviewed, and is explicitly a methods/small-data study rather than a debt-model improvement — [Theseus](https://theseus.fi/handle/10024/861416).
- Two arXiv deep-learning papers are adjacent but not debt-path forecasts: one builds data-driven macroeconomic fan charts with deep learning for US GDP growth, inflation, and unemployment (216 input series) rather than debt-to-GDP — content not verified beyond search snippet, PDF text was not machine-readable via fetch — [arXiv 2204.06848](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.06848). A second, "Macroeconomic forecasting and sovereign risk assessment using deep learning techniques" (arXiv 2301.09856), reportedly uses a deep neural network on US monthly data (1973-2018) with government debt/GDP among nine inputs, framed around sovereign risk — this is an arXiv preprint, not confirmed peer-reviewed, and full text could not be extracted (PDF binary unreadable by fetch tool), so findings are unverified beyond the search-result description.
- Alaminos et al. (*Symmetry*, 2021) — just outside the 2022-2026 window but frequently cited as the benchmark predecessor — found fuzzy decision trees, AdaBoost, XGBoost, and deep-learning neural decision trees outperformed logistic/statistical models for sovereign debt crisis prediction specifically (random forests did better for currency crises) — secondary-summary level only.
- Older SVM-vs-neural-network comparisons (2006) for debt-to-GDP forecasting, where SVM beat a back-propagation neural network on MAE/MAPE/MSE/RMSE, were surfaced but are too old to be in scope — noted only to flag that this exact comparison is not new.

### Inferences
- The genuinely new ML material (2022-2026) is concentrated in binary/early-warning crisis classification (will country X enter distress / an IMF program / default), not in generating a continuous stochastic debt-path distribution. This means ML is not yet a drop-in replacement for the Monte Carlo debt-accounting engine the existing model uses — it answers a different question (classification risk) rather than producing fan charts.
- Results are genuinely mixed, not uniformly positive: Bank of Italy/Open Economies Review and a FinTech-journal paper report ML beating probit/logit, while a World Bank working paper reports the reverse for debt distress specifically, and the FinTech paper flags overfitting in its best (XGBoost) model. This supports treating ML performance claims with real skepticism rather than as settled.
- Much of the sourcing here is thin — one key positive result (Bank of Italy) and the key negative counter-result (World Bank) were both read at abstract/snippet level only, not full text, because full-text retrieval failed or was paywalled; a report-writer citing specific AUC/accuracy numbers from these papers should flag that the exact figures were not independently verified in this pass.

## Are there Bayesian model-averaging or Bayesian VAR approaches to debt-sustainability analysis specifically, as opposed to simpler parameter-sampling-from-ranges?

### Takeaway
Yes — Bayesian VAR (BVAR), not classical Bayesian model-averaging across competing structural models, is the concrete and recent (2025) development here: both a Banque de France working paper and an ESM brief now build debt-sustainability fan charts from BVARs (with priors) rather than plain historical-shock bootstraps or independent parameter sampling, and the Banque de France paper explicitly shows the Bayesian version produces modestly wider, more conservative uncertainty bands than the frequentist VAR. True BMA (averaging across several different DSA model specifications with posterior model weights) was not found applied to debt-sustainability analysis itself; the BMA literature that does exist uses BMA to select growth/debt *drivers*, not to average competing debt-path models.

### Cited Findings
- Baret, Bec & Cochard, "Quantifying Uncertainty in France's Debt Trajectory: A VAR Based Analysis," Banque de France Working Paper Series no. 1019, published 14 November 2025 (full text fetched). The paper runs a quarterly VAR (1990Q1-2023Q4) on the primary balance, nominal GDP growth, and short- and long-term nominal interest rates, generating 10,000 simulated paths combined with the debt-accounting identity to produce debt/GDP fan charts, for both a standard frequentist VAR and a Bayesian VAR (BVAR) with mild informative priors — [Banque de France](https://www.banque-france.fr/en/publications-and-statistics/publications/quantifying-uncertainty-frances-debt-trajectory-var-based-analysis).
- In that paper, the BVAR's 10-90th percentile fan cone for 2028 is modestly wider than the frequentist VAR's, and the BVAR gives a somewhat *lower* probability that debt stays above its 2023 level by 2028 than the frequentist version does; both place their overall uncertainty bands between those separately reported by the European Commission and the ECB — [Banque de France, full text read](https://www.banque-france.fr/en/publications-and-statistics/publications/quantifying-uncertainty-frances-debt-trajectory-var-based-analysis).
- An ESM Brief (no. 5, "Debt sustainability under uncertainty," dated/filed November 2025) was identified as describing a similar pipeline — a quarterly BVAR with seven variables generating simulations adjusted toward a baseline forecast — but the PDF's text could not be machine-extracted by the fetch tool (binary/compressed stream), so this is reported at search-snippet level only, not confirmed by direct reading — [ESM](https://www.esm.europa.eu/system/files/document/2025-11/ESM%20Brief%205%20-%20Debt%20sustainability%20under%20uncertainty.pdf).
- Nagou et al. (2021, just outside the 2022-2026 window but the closest BMA-adjacent precedent) use Bayesian model averaging on 51 African countries (1990-2018) across 27 candidate variables to identify robust drivers of debt accumulation (foreign aid, fiscal deficits, trade openness, debt-service costs) — this is BMA for *determinants* of debt, not BMA across competing debt-sustainability forecasting models — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/oaefxx/v9y2021i1p1860282.html), abstract-level.

### Inferences
- The field's "Bayesian" innovation for DSA specifically is BVAR-based fan-chart construction (adding priors to a VAR used for simulation), not classical BMA over multiple candidate debt models. For a model that currently treats each structural parameter as independently drawn from a literature range, the nearest-term, best-evidenced upgrade path is adopting a VAR (frequentist or Bayesian) over the historical-shock bootstrap, since this simultaneously (a) correlates the shocks instead of treating them independently and (b) is exactly what two 2025 institutional papers (Banque de France, ESM) just did for France/euro-area DSA.
- The fact that BVAR gives *wider* bands than frequentist VAR in the Banque de France result is a useful, specific, citable data point for arguing that adding parameter uncertainty (via priors) widens — not narrows — the resulting fan chart relative to a plug-in VAR, which is relevant context if the existing model is deciding whether a VAR upgrade would be more or less conservative than current parameter-range sampling.

### Gaps
- The ESM Brief 5 document could not be read in full text (PDF extraction failed); its description of variable count, prior structure, and how it "adjusts simulations toward a baseline forecast" is from search-engine snippets only, not independently verified — a report-writer should caveat any specific claim about this brief as secondary/unverified.
- No genuine Bayesian-model-averaging-across-competing-DSA-models paper (as distinct from BVAR-for-simulation or BMA-for-determinants) was found in the 2022-2026 window; this may be a true gap in the literature rather than a search failure, but confidence here is moderate given search depth achieved.
- Could not confirm whether either the Banque de France or ESM approach has been applied outside advanced-economy (France/euro area) contexts; no emerging-market BVAR-DSA application was found in this pass (see also the EM-specific question below, where a quantile-regression VAR for Brazil/Mexico/Turkey was found but not a Bayesian one).

## Has anyone built a joint/correlated stochastic shock model using copulas for fiscal variables (growth, interest rates, primary balance, exchange rate), as opposed to independent sampling or linear-VAR correlation?

### Takeaway
Copula-based dependence modelling is well established in the sovereign-risk literature, but overwhelmingly for *market-priced* sovereign/financial default risk (CDS spreads, sovereign bond yields, systemic contagion) rather than for the underlying *macro-fiscal* variables (growth, interest rate, primary balance, FX) that feed a debt-accounting identity; no 2022-2026 (or earlier) paper was found that builds a copula specifically over growth/interest-rate/primary-balance/FX for Monte Carlo debt-path simulation. This is a credible, reportable gap: the technique exists and is mature in adjacent finance literature, but its specific application to DSA-style fiscal-variable joint simulation does not appear to have been done, at least not surfaced by this search.

### Cited Findings
- Lucas, Schwaab & Zhang (Sveriges Riksbank working paper) estimate joint and conditional sovereign default probabilities from euro-area CDS prices, comparing Gaussian, symmetric-t, skewed-t, and a dynamic skewed-t copula; they find the choice of dependence/distributional assumption matters most for *conditional* risk estimates, and that dependence between sovereigns is time-varying with spillovers — abstract/secondary-summary level, not full text — [AEA conference paper](https://aeaweb.org/conference/2014/retrieve.php?pdfid=16). (Note: this working paper predates the 2022-2026 window in its original form but is the methodological anchor for later copula-sovereign work; cited here only as context, not as a "recent" finding.)
- Reboredo & Ugolini (2015, *North American Journal of Economics and Finance*) use CoVaR built with copulas/vine copulas to show systemic financial-sector impact of Greek sovereign distress rose for Greece, Italy, and Portugal during the Greek crisis — older paper, cited only as the methodological lineage for vine-copula sovereign work; abstract-level — [Unimib repository](https://boa.unimib.it/handle/10281/334021).
- A 2025 article, "The Global Financial Cycle and country risk in emerging markets during stress episodes: A Copula-CoVaR approach" (*Research in International Business and Finance*, vol. 73, 2025) was identified by title/venue only; full content and specific findings could not be confirmed in this pass (title-only, not read) — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/riibaf/v73y2025ipas0275531924003945.html). This is the most recent (2025) copula-sovereign-risk paper found and may be worth a follow-up read, since it is explicitly emerging-market-focused.
- No paper was found using a copula (vine or otherwise) to jointly simulate the macro-fiscal *inputs* to a debt-accounting identity (growth, interest rate, primary balance, FX) for stochastic DSA purposes. Repeated targeted searches ("copula primary balance interest rate growth exchange rate," "vine copula macro-fiscal variables sovereign risk simulation 2023 2024 2025") returned only CDS-spread/financial-contagion copula applications, none of which model the primary balance or growth directly as copula marginals for debt-path simulation.
- Bank of England WP (Hurd, Salmon & Schleicher, 2007) uses copulas for bivariate FX-rate distributions — too old and FX-only (no fiscal variables), cited only to show the copula-for-FX technique itself is not new; abstract-level.

### Inferences
- Given that copula methods are mature and well-validated for correlating sovereign-risk/market variables, but apparently never applied to the growth/rate/primary-balance/FX bundle that feeds a debt-accounting identity, a copula-based joint shock model is a methodologically available but seemingly unexploited upgrade for a model currently using independent parameter sampling plus separate bootstraps. This would be a genuinely novel contribution if implemented, not merely catching up to an established technique — which is a different framing than the ML or BVAR findings above (where the model would be adopting an already-institutionally-tested method).
- The absence of hits despite several query variations increases confidence this is a real literature gap rather than a search-tooling failure, but this is not certain given the 15-tool-call budget constraint of this research pass.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm whether the 2025 Copula-CoVaR emerging-market paper (RIBAF vol. 73) uses any fiscal-accounting variables (vs. purely market/price variables); this would need a direct full-text read to settle.
- Could not determine whether any central-bank or IMF/World Bank internal (non-published or very recent) DSA toolkit uses copulas; institutional working-paper series (ECB, BIS) were not exhaustively searched for this specific technique within the tool-call budget.
- No non-euro-area / non-CDS copula application to fiscal risk was found at all; if one exists in a currently-indexed-but-unsurfaced working paper, this pass did not find it.

## Is there recent work (2022-2026) on climate-related or other tail-risk/contingent-liability fiscal risks incorporated into stochastic debt projections?

### Takeaway
Yes, and this is the strongest, best-evidenced finding of the whole survey: the IMF has an explicit, named stochastic tool for this — the Natural Disaster Debt Dynamics Tool (ND-DDT) — which was directly applied and documented in a November 2025 academic paper on Croatia, giving concrete, citable quantitative results on how disaster shocks shift a Monte-Carlo debt fan chart; this is structurally the closest analogue in the literature to the existing model's "major-corruption shock" stochastic process, just for climate/disaster risk instead. Separately, the IMF has institutionalized climate risk into its own Debt Sustainability Framework methodology (2022 climate modules in the Market-Access Countries Sovereign Risk and DSA framework; a 2024 LIC-DSF climate supplement), and financial-sector contingent-liability stochastic/micro-simulation work (the EU's SYMBOL model) exists as a separate, mature strand, though its most clearly-dated application found here is pre-2022.

### Cited Findings
- Draganić, Srdelić & Davila-Fernandez, "Extreme events and public debt dynamics: Lessons from Croatia's experience" (arXiv:2511.02973v1, November 2025; full text fetched). The paper applies the IMF's Natural Disaster Debt Dynamic Tool (ND-DDT, attributed to Acosta-Ormaechea et al., exact IMF publication not independently located/confirmed in this pass) to Croatia. Methodology: disaster shocks hit real GDP growth and the primary balance; two stochastic configurations are used — a single first-year shock, and a configuration drawing shocks from the empirical disaster-impact distribution in every projection year, both calibrated to the 5th percentile of historical outcomes; uncertainty itself is captured via a Monte Carlo fan chart with 10,000 iterations drawing macro-fiscal variables from a multivariate normal distribution calibrated to historical means/covariances — methodologically very close to the existing model's own Monte Carlo/bootstrap structure — [arXiv, full text read](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02973v1).
- Quantitative results from that paper: baseline Croatian debt/GDP falls from ~87.7% (2020) to ~55-56% by 2030-31; a one-off disaster shock (calibrated to the 2020 Zagreb earthquake, ~11.7% of GDP in damage) pushes debt up ~8 points by 2026 and to ~71.7% by 2031 (~16 points above the no-disaster path); repeated/recurring disaster shocks push debt to ~73.2% by 2031; a higher pre-disaster primary surplus (+3% of GDP) and stronger "adaptive capacity" reduce the post-shock debt peak by ~3-4 points and shorten recovery time by ~2 years — [arXiv, full text read](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02973v1). The authors flag that an alternative specification adding interest-rate and inflation shocks produces implausible debt levels (~150% of GDP) and explicitly warn these specific results are unreliable — a useful honesty flag worth replicating in any downstream report.
- Akanbi, Gbohoui & Lam, IMF Technical Notes and Manuals 2023-002, "Calibrating Fiscal Rules: A Consideration of Natural Disaster Risks" — extends IMF fiscal-rule calibration toolkits to incorporate disaster shocks, with accompanying toolkits — found via title/abstract only, not fetched in full — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imftnm/2023-002.html).
- IMF's DIGNAD model (Debt, Investment, Growth, and Natural Disasters) is a general-equilibrium toolkit (distinct from ND-DDT) for analyzing how disasters and resilience investment affect debt and growth — found via secondary aggregator page, not an IMF primary source; not independently confirmed in full — [Open Net Zero](https://opennetzero.org/the-international-monetary-fund-imf/dignad).
- IMF introduced dedicated climate-change modules within its Market Access Countries Sovereign Risk and Debt Sustainability Framework in 2022, and issued a July 2024 guidance-note supplement extending similar climate treatment to the Low-Income Country Debt Sustainability Framework (LIC-DSF) — reported via a 2026 Finance Watch position paper's secondary description, not read from the primary IMF document itself — [Finance Watch](https://www.finance-watch.org/policy-portal/public-finance/fiscal-sustainability-in-a-changing-climate/).
- Marto, Papageorgiou & Klyuev, IMF WP 17/223 (2017, outside the 2022-2026 window but foundational) model debt sustainability concerns from post-disaster infrastructure rebuilding in small developing states, comparing ex ante buffers/adaptation investment with post-disaster donor support — cited only as lineage, abstract-level, not a "recent" finding — [IMF](https://imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WP/2017/wp17223.ashx).
- Cebotari & Youssef, IMF WP 20/3 (2020, also just outside window) on sovereign natural-disaster insurance as a contingent-liability/fiscal-risk-management tool for smaller, lower-income states — abstract-level, cited as lineage only.
- On financial-sector contingent liabilities specifically: the EU's SYMBOL model (European Commission JRC/DG FISMA) micro-simulates how bank losses could reach public budgets once capital, bail-in, and resolution funds are exhausted, concluding residual costs to governments appear limited post-2008-reform, with country variation — found via an EU quarterly report entry, exact publication year could not be confirmed as being within 2022-2026 (may be older), abstract-level only — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/euf/qreuro/0223-02.html).
- A 2025 study of "multiple disasters and debt sustainability in Small Island Developing States" (1980-2018 sample) found only a weak correlation between a severe natural disaster and external debt, attributed to highly-indebted SIDS' limited financing access — this is a useful negative/nuancing finding, but read at search-snippet level only, not full text — [PreventionWeb](https://preventionweb.net/quick/51307).

### Inferences
- The ND-DDT/Croatia paper is the single most directly transplantable finding for the existing model: it is essentially "the same Monte Carlo debt-accounting approach the existing model already uses, plus one additional named stochastic shock channel (disaster risk hitting growth and primary balance, calibrated to the empirical disaster-impact distribution or a single first-year tail event)." This is structurally parallel to the model's existing "major-corruption shock" process, strongly supporting the idea that a climate/disaster-shock channel is a natural, literature-validated extension rather than a speculative one.
- The explicit IMF institutionalization of climate risk into both the Market-Access and LIC debt-sustainability frameworks (2022 and 2024 respectively) signals this is no longer a fringe or academic-only concern but a mainstream methodological expectation for sovereign DSA as of the 2022-2026 period — relevant if the paper is aiming to be read as current/credible by DSA practitioners.
- The honest caveat from the Croatia paper (implausible ~150% debt/GDP results from an alternative specification, which the authors themselves flag as unreliable) is a good illustration of the real methodological fragility of disaster-shock DSA extensions and should be cited to avoid overselling how straightforward this extension is.

### Gaps
- Could not locate and directly read the IMF's own primary ND-DDT documentation/working paper (only a secondary citation, "Acosta-Ormaechea et al. (2025)," as referenced inside the Croatia paper); exact IMF publication details (title, WP number, date) are unconfirmed.
- Could not confirm the SYMBOL-model publication's exact year, so cannot certify it falls within the 2022-2026 window; flagged as likely-older but relevant background.
- No contingent-liability stochastic DSA paper specifically combining a *financial-sector* shock channel with a full Monte Carlo debt fan chart (as opposed to either a separate micro-simulation exercise or a disaster-only DSA tool) was found within the 2022-2026 window — this may be a genuine gap or simply not surfaced given the tool-call budget.
- The IMF's own 2022 climate-module methodology paper for the Market Access Countries Sovereign Risk and DSA framework was not directly read (only described second-hand via Finance Watch); a report-writer wanting the precise mechanics should flag this as needing primary-source verification.

## Recent (2023-2026) emerging-market-specific debt sustainability papers using a notably different/more advanced approach than classical VAR/fan-chart DSA?

### Takeaway
