# AI-Disclosure Norms in Economics Journals (2023-2026), with Focus on Fiscal Studies/Wiley

## Q1: Does Wiley / the Institute for Fiscal Studies have a published, findable AI-disclosure policy for submitted manuscripts? What does it say?

### Takeaway
Wiley has a publisher-wide generative-AI policy for journal authors (plus a separate, newer one for book authors, released March 2025) that requires disclosure of substantive AI use "in the Methods section or, where a Methods section is not available, in a suitable alternative part of the manuscript (e.g., cover letter, Acknowledgements section, or another appropriate section)." I could not find a *Fiscal Studies*-specific or Institute for Fiscal Studies-specific AI policy page distinct from Wiley's general policy — none of my searches surfaced one, so I cannot confirm Fiscal Studies has bespoke language beyond inheriting Wiley's house policy.

### Cited Findings
- Wiley publicly released "AI guidelines for authors" in March 2025 (announced at the London Book Fair), developed after interviews with authors, editors, and AI experts; this is described as a complement to Wiley's pre-existing generative-AI framework for journal publications — i.e., the journal-level policy predates March 2025 and the 2025 release is additional/book-focused guidance — [UKSG](https://www.uksg.org/?p=21103), [Wiley newsroom press release](https://newsroom.wiley.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2025/Wiley-Releases-AI-Guidelines-for-Authors/default.aspx).
- A third-party mirror of Wiley's journal-author policy (Enago) states: AI use in developing manuscript content (including drafting and translation) "must be described, transparently and in detail" in the Methods section, a disclosure statement, or Acknowledgements; light grammar/spelling tools are exempt from disclosure; generative AI must not be used "to create, alter, or manipulate original research data or results including data visualization, illustrations and photographs" — [Enago: Wiley AI Guidelines](https://www.enago.com/responsible-ai-movement/publisher-ai-guidelines/wiley-ai-guidelines/).
- An earlier, dated Wiley "Generative AI Policy" for authors is referenced as of September 2023 by CASRAI — [CASRAI: AI in Manuscripts Publisher Policy Landscape](https://www.casrai.org/guides/ai-in-manuscripts-publisher-policy-landscape).
- A 2023 academic bibliometric survey of publisher instructions-to-authors (covering Wiley among others) found that in that era, policy language treated AI tools as unable to be authors and required disclosure of AI use "to help generate content, write code, or analyze data" in cover letters and in the Methods/Acknowledgements — this exact phrasing was attributed to Oxford University Press rather than Wiley in the search result, but illustrates that some publishers explicitly extended disclosure duty to code/data analysis, not just prose, as early as 2023 — [arXiv 2307.11918, Bibliometric Analysis of Publisher and Journal AI Instructions](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.11918).
- I did not find any Wiley or Institute for Fiscal Studies page specifically naming *Fiscal Studies* (the journal) and its own AI-disclosure rule; searches for "Fiscal Studies journal instructions for authors AI policy" returned only unrelated journals (Financial Management Perspective, Taylor & Francis's Public Money & Management, Elsevier's Economic Analysis and Policy) and no Fiscal Studies-specific or IFS-specific page.

### Inferences
- Fiscal Studies almost certainly falls under Wiley's general journal-wide generative-AI policy (disclosure in Methods/Acknowledgements/disclosure statement; no AI authorship; no AI-generated data/figures) by default, since Wiley applies house-wide ethics policies across its portfolio and I found no evidence of a journal-specific carve-out.
- Because the exact, current, authoritative Wiley policy text could not be fetched directly from a Wiley-owned domain (only third-party mirrors, some with conflicting "last updated" dates in 2026), the precise current wording should be treated as approximate, not verbatim-confirmed.

### Gaps
- No Fiscal Studies-specific or IFS-specific AI policy page was found or ruled out with certainty; it is possible one exists but wasn't surfaced by search (e.g., buried in a journal-specific "Author Guidelines" subpage on Wiley Online Library that my searches didn't reach, given a WebFetch session-limit interruption prevented a direct fetch of authorservices.wiley.com).
- Could not verify the single authoritative current-version Wiley policy text directly from wiley.com; all wording is via third-party summaries (Enago, CASRAI, UKSG), which is a meaningfully weaker source quality than a primary document.

## Q2: What do major economics-adjacent publishers (Elsevier, Springer, OUP, AEA, Econometric Society) say, and is there an emerging 2026 consensus on required disclosure content?

### Takeaway
There is a clear, converging consensus across all surveyed publishers/societies on two points: (1) AI tools cannot be listed as an author, and (2) authors are fully accountable for verifying any AI-assisted content. There is more variation on *what must be disclosed and where* — most policies focus on AI use in writing/editing, with Elsevier, Springer, and OUP explicit that AI must not create/alter research data, while a few finance/economics-adjacent outlets (the Journal of Finance, under the American Finance Association) go further and impose specific, stricter technical disclosure requirements for AI used in data/code/analysis (open-source or time-stamped models, documented prompts) — distinct from the writing-disclosure norm.

### Cited Findings
- **Elsevier**: journal guide-for-authors language (per CASRAI summary, not Elsevier's own page directly) requires authors to declare generative AI use in preparing a manuscript, with the statement placed just before the reference list; basic grammar/spell-check is exempt — [CASRAI: Elsevier generative AI authorship policy](https://casrai.org/guides/elsevier-generative-ai-authorship-policy). Elsevier's own "Generative AI Policies for Books and Commissioned Content" pages (confirmed Elsevier-owned domain) state AI may be used pre-submission only to improve language/readability, with disclosure, and ban AI-generated artwork/graphical abstracts — [Elsevier Generative AI Policies](https://www.elsevier.com/about/policies-and-standards/publishing-ethics-books/generative-ai-policies).
- **Springer Nature**: its editorial policy (confirmed Springer-owned domain) states LLMs do not meet authorship criteria because authorship requires accountability; LLM use "should be properly documented in the Methods section (and if a Methods section is not available, in a suitable alternative part of the manuscript)"; narrowly exempts "AI assisted copy editing" for grammar/style/spelling of human-written text; reviewers are told not to upload manuscripts into generative AI tools — [Springer Nature AI editorial policy](https://www.springer.com/us/editorial-policies/artificial-intelligence--ai-/25428500).
- **Oxford University Press**: per a 2023 bibliometric study, OUP's policy required disclosure of AI use "to help generate content, write code, or analyze data" in both cover letters and Methods/Acknowledgements — notably this is one of the only policies found that explicitly names "write code" as a disclosable AI use alongside content generation and data analysis — [arXiv 2307.11918](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.11918). A 2026 third-party (Enago) summary describes OUP as requiring, for non-language uses, "a detailed statement at manuscript submission describing where and how each tool was used, the tool and version, the justification, and the steps taken to validate output accuracy," and that generative AI should not perform "primary author responsibilities" (generating arguments, writing analysis, drawing conclusions) — [Enago: OUP AI Guidelines](https://www.enago.com/responsible-ai-movement/publisher-ai-guidelines/OUP-ai-guidelines).
- **American Economic Association (AER, AEJ series, AER:Insights)**: AEA's own submission pages state AI software "may not be listed as an author"; if AI helped prepare a manuscript (drafting/editing text) "this must be briefly described during the submission process"; authors are "solely accountable for, and must thoroughly fact-check, outputs created with the help of AI software." This policy is about text drafting/editing and does not explicitly extend to code or data analysis in the language found — [AEA journal submissions pages, e.g. aeaweb.org/journals/aer/submissions].
- **Econometric Society (Econometrica, Quantitative Economics)**: the "AI Policy" section of Econometrica's Editorial Procedures and Policies states authors may use AI tools but "remain responsible and accountable for all content of their work," covering review/verification of AI output, all analysis/interpretations/ideas, and safeguarding data privacy/IP; AI systems cannot be listed as authors. Critically, **this section does not distinguish AI use in writing from AI use in code/data analysis, and it does not impose an affirmative disclosure requirement** (i.e., it's a responsibility/accountability clause, not a "you must disclose AI use" clause). The separate "Disclosure Policy" section (financial support, board positions, IRB approval, pre-review rights) does not mention AI at all — [Econometric Society, Econometrica Editorial Procedures and Policies](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/editorial-procedures-and-policies).
- **American Finance Association / Journal of Finance** (finance, not strictly AEA, but close economics-adjacent peer): per its Editorial Policies page, writing/code-quality AI assistance just needs brief description at submission (no AI co-authorship; full author fact-checking responsibility) — but **AI used to create new datasets, classify data, or conduct analysis is subject to stricter rules**: authors must use open-source AI models or "clearly time-stamped versions of proprietary models," and must provide "detailed documentation of the prompts used to create the data," explicitly framed as serving replicability and spreading best practice. Referees may use AI to draft/edit reports or search references but remain accountable — [AFA Editorial Policies](https://afajof.org/editorial-policies/). This is the clearest, most concrete economics/finance-adjacent precedent found for treating AI-in-analysis disclosure as categorically stricter/different from AI-in-writing disclosure.
- **COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics)**, which many of the above reference or align with: position statement (first published 13 Feb 2023) says AI tools cannot be credited as authors (lack accountability, cannot hold copyright/declare conflicts of interest); authors using AI for writing, images, or data work "should name the tool and describe how it was used," typically in Materials and Methods or similar; authors remain fully liable for all content including AI-generated parts — [COPE position statement](https://publicationethics.org/guidance/cope-position/authorship-and-ai-tools).

### Inferences
- As of late 2026, the consensus "floor" across economics-adjacent publishers is: (a) no AI authorship, (b) author full accountability/fact-checking duty, (c) disclosure required for substantive AI contributions to text, typically in Methods/Acknowledgements/disclosure statement/cover letter. This is well-established and uncontroversial.
- A disclosure-of-AI-in-code-or-analysis norm is emerging but is less mature, less uniform, and enforced unevenly: AEA and Econometric Society policy text (the two most central pure-economics bodies) does not explicitly call out code/data-analysis AI use as a distinct disclosable category — it is folded into general "accountability" language. OUP (2023 wording) and AFA/Journal of Finance (current wording) are the two found instances that explicitly carve out AI-in-code/data-analysis as warranting *more detailed* disclosure (tool+version, prompts, timestamps) than AI-in-writing. This suggests the field is heading toward requiring that level of detail, but it is not yet the norm everywhere, and most-cited pure-economics venues (AER, Econometrica) have not yet caught up to the Journal of Finance's level of specificity.
- Given this, a paper that discloses specific coding-agent names, operating mode ("under direct and continuous human direction"), and verification practice is going *beyond* what AER/Econometrica currently require, but is consistent with — and arguably exactly the kind of detail anticipated by — the stricter end of the spectrum (OUP 2023 language, AFA/JF current language, COPE's general "name the tool and describe how it was used").

### Gaps
- Could not fetch Elsevier's or OUP's own primary policy page directly (only third-party mirrors/summaries and one older bibliometric paper); exact current wording unverified against a primary Elsevier/OUP source.
- Could not confirm whether the AEA has updated its AI policy specifically to address code/data-analysis AI use (as AFA/JF has); only confirmed the writing-disclosure clause.
- No access to COPE's "last reviewed" date beyond initial Feb 2023 publication — could not confirm if it has been revised since.

## Q3: What is the TYPICAL level of detail in actual 2024-2026 economics/public-finance journal AI-disclosure statements? Does any published paper match the described level of detail?

### Takeaway
The typical disclosure found in practice — across general academic publishing commentary and the handful of concrete economics-adjacent examples located — is a short, one-to-a-few-sentence statement naming a tool (e.g., "ChatGPT," "GPT-4," "Claude") and a narrow use (editing/language polish, or programming help), with a boilerplate responsibility clause. I did not find any *published, peer-reviewed* economics or public-finance journal article (as opposed to a working paper/preprint) with a disclosure statement anywhere near the length, specificity, and three-part structure (coding-agent methodology + AI-assisted literature search + AI-assisted prose) described in the user's paper. The closest analogues found are in computer-science/ML preprints, not economics journals.

### Cited Findings
- A December 2025 preprint analyzing journal AI policies found an "upward trend" in AI-characteristic keyword density in papers beginning in 2023, "exceeding 30 AI-characteristic keywords per paper by 2025," and argues that disclosure policies have not curbed AI-assisted writing — a prevalence/compliance finding, not itself a disclosure-content example — [arXiv 2512.06705](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06705).
- A bioethics-scholarship study found generative-AI disclosure in that field's published literature to be rare — observed self-disclosure rate under 1% of articles reviewed — suggesting that even where a stated policy exists, actual disclosure in published economics/social-science articles may be uncommon, and the modal paper that does use AI simply doesn't disclose it at all (true baseline for "typical" may be *zero disclosure*, not a short one) — [PhilArchive: "The missing disclosure: is generative AI use in bioethics scholarship going largely unreported?"](https://philarchive.org/rec/BOBTMD).
- A 2025/2026 arXiv paper in applied economics, "An Auditable AI Agent Loop for Empirical Economics: A Case Study in Forecast Combination" (Minchul Shin), is reported (via search snippet) to include "a declaration that the author used two named AI models for editing and programming help, and that the author reviewed the output and takes responsibility for it" — this is a short, named-tool-plus-responsibility-clause disclosure, i.e., typical brevity, though notably it does cover "programming help" specifically, similar in kind (though shorter) to the code-development disclosure in question — [arXiv 2603.17381](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17381) (exact statement text could not be extracted; PDF content was binary-encoded and unreadable via fetch).
- Anton Korinek (University of Virginia/GovAI) runs a recurring "Generative AI for Economic Research" feature in the Journal of Economic Literature, indicating that JEL — a top-tier AEA journal — has an established, recurring editorial channel specifically about AI's role in economic research methodology; this is a signal that top economics journals are actively engaging with AI-in-research-methods as a topic, though I could not retrieve the actual disclosure practices discussed within that series — [search snippets; primary JEL series not directly fetched].
- Outside economics, in computer science, detailed code-development AI disclosures do exist and are becoming normalized: e.g., a 2026 arXiv paper on AI disclosure in CS research itself discloses "Claude Code was used to write and edit code for parallelizing its disclosure-extraction pipeline... All generated code was manually verified for correctness" — a single-sentence, narrow version of the same genre of disclosure — [arXiv 2608.23271, "Expectations and Practices around AI Disclosure in CS Research"]. Another CS/ML preprint on coding agents replicating scientific papers has a dedicated "Declaration of generative AI" section and releases full agent prompts/workspaces for both Codex and Claude Code — a notably more extensive disclosure, comparable in spirit (full methodological transparency about agent use) to the paper in question, but still in CS/ML, not economics — [arXiv 2607.02134].
- No Fiscal Studies article, no AER/AEJ/Econometrica article, and no NBER working paper was found in these searches with a disclosure statement matching the three-part structure (extensive AI-coded 15k-line codebase + AI-assisted literature search + AI-assisted prose) described in the task. Search for a 2024 NBER paper using "ChatGPT was used" in acknowledgments returned no exact match; the closest NBER hit (WP 34255, "How People Use ChatGPT") is a paper *about* ChatGPT usage data, not a paper *disclosing* ChatGPT use in its own production.

### Inferences
- The typical published economics-journal AI disclosure as of 2026, where present at all, is brief and writing/editing-focused ("AI was used to improve language/readability of this manuscript"), consistent with what COPE/AEA/Econometric Society policies actually require as a floor.
- Disclosure of AI use in *literature search* specifically (part (b) of the user's paper's disclosure) appears to have essentially no precedent found in economics journals at all — none of the searches surfaced any economics paper disclosing AI-assisted literature search as a distinct, named practice. This appears to be a genuinely novel/unusual disclosure category in this literature, not just an unusually detailed version of a common one.
- Disclosure of AI use in *extensive, named-agent, codebase-scale development* (part (a)) likewise has no found economics-journal precedent; the nearest analogues are CS/ML preprints (arXiv, not peer-reviewed econ journals) and one applied-economics arXiv working paper with only a brief "programming help" mention — an order of magnitude less detailed than naming specific coding agents and describing continuous-direction methodology.
- Taken together, the paper's disclosure statement is best characterized as a hybrid: its *category* (writing assistance) is squarely normal and expected; its other two categories (code-development methodology, literature-search methodology) are categories publishers' current policies gesture toward covering ("write code," "analyze data" per OUP 2023 wording; AFA/JF's prompts/timestamps requirement) but that, in practice, essentially no published economics paper yet discloses with this level of narrative detail.

## Q4: Is there a documented norm (or any documented case) specifically for disclosing AI-ASSISTED CODE/MODEL DEVELOPMENT in economics or adjacent empirical social science — distinct from writing disclosure?

### Takeaway
Honestly: there is very little documented, economics-specific norm for this yet. The clearest concrete precedent found is the Journal of Finance's (AFA) current editorial policy, which explicitly treats AI-in-data/code/analysis as a *separate, stricter* disclosure category from AI-in-writing (requiring open-source or time-stamped proprietary models and documented prompts for data/analysis uses) — but this is one finance journal's policy text, not a demonstrated case of a published article actually using it, and not a field-wide norm. Beyond that, the evidence is policy-statement-level (OUP's 2023 "write code" language, COPE's generic "data work" language) rather than a body of actual-practice examples. I found no case of a published economics paper with an extensive, codebase-scale AI-coding disclosure comparable to what is described in the user's paper.

### Cited Findings
- Journal of Finance (AFA): as cited in Q2, imposes a distinct, stricter disclosure requirement for AI used in data creation/classification/analysis (time-stamped/open-source models + documented prompts) versus the lighter "briefly describe" requirement for AI-assisted writing/code-quality editing — [AFA Editorial Policies](https://afajof.org/editorial-policies/). Note this talks about data/analysis, and separately mentions "AI to draft or edit text or improve code quality" bundled with writing-level disclosure (brief description) — so even within this one policy, "AI improves code quality" is treated as the lighter-disclosure writing-like category, while "AI creates data/conducts analysis" is the stricter category. This is a meaningful nuance: the Journal of Finance's own taxonomy doesn't map cleanly onto "all code-development AI use requires detailed disclosure" — it depends on whether the AI involvement affects the data/results versus just the code's quality/tooling.
- OUP's 2023-era policy (per bibliometric study) is the only general publisher policy found that puts "write code" in the same breath as "generate content" and "analyze data" as a disclosable AI use requiring Methods/Acknowledgements description — [arXiv 2307.11918](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.11918).
- COPE's general guidance says AI used for "data work" should be named and its use described — but does not elaborate on code-development specifically, nor on the distinction between incidental coding help (e.g., autocomplete) versus the kind of extensive, continuous, agentic code-development described in the user's paper — [COPE position statement](https://publicationethics.org/guidance/cope-position/authorship-and-ai-tools).
- An NBER working paper by Anton Korinek (2025, per search snippet) explicitly discusses how economists can build LLM-based agents to write and debug econometric code, and a VoxEU/CEPR column reports a controlled-benchmark finding that letting an AI agent run and iterate on its own code raised the share of correct results from 79% to 97% across a 40-task benchmark, while flagging that "wrong-but-error-free" outputs remain a risk researchers must independently catch — this is relevant *methodological* context (economists are actively studying AI-agent code generation as a research tool) but is not itself a disclosure-norm statement — [CEPR/VoxEU, "Letting AI agents write econometric code"](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/letting-ai-agents-write-econometric-code); NBER working paper reference via search snippet, not independently verified.
- The VoxEU column "Plus ça change: AI and the future of economic research" argues AI tools should be brought into the *same* disclosure framework used for other research inputs, and separately recommends that journals move toward *stricter* requirements for publishing code/data/methodological documentation generally (not AI-specific) — i.e., this is a normative call/argument from a commentator, not evidence of an existing norm — [CEPR/VoxEU column](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/plus-ca-change-ai-and-future-economic-research-galileo-back-socrates).
- Outside economics, in CS/ML, there is a more developed practice of disclosing agentic-coding-tool use (Claude Code, Codex) in papers' own development, including a dedicated "Declaration of generative AI" section with full released prompts in at least one 2026 arXiv preprint — [arXiv 2607.02134]. This exists as a CS-field precedent/template but has not (per these searches) migrated into economics-journal practice.

### Inferences
- There is essentially no established, field-specific economics norm yet for exactly this scenario (an entire ~15,000-line simulation codebase developed iteratively by named AI coding agents under continuous human direction). The closest thing to an applicable norm is "disclose AI use in code/data work, in the Methods/Acknowledgements, naming the tool," generically inherited from COPE/OUP/AFA-style language — but no one has yet calibrated what disclosure *should* look like for an AI-built codebase of this scale, because (as far as this research could find) no published economics paper has yet done this at this scale with this level of disclosure detail.
- This is the correct honest conclusion per the task's own expectation (question 4 explicitly anticipates this is "the least likely dimension to have an established norm yet") — the research supports that expectation rather than contradicting it.

### Gaps
- No case was found — positive or negative — of a specific published economics or adjacent empirical social-science paper being challenged, criticized, or flagged by reviewers/editors specifically over an AI-code-development disclosure. This absence could reflect either (a) no such case has occurred, (b) such cases exist but aren't indexed/discussed publicly, or (c) search tooling simply didn't surface it. Cannot distinguish between these given available evidence.
- Could not verify the Korinek NBER paper's exact content on agent-written econometric code (reference came via a search-engine paraphrase, not a direct primary-source read).
- Did not locate any economics-specific professional body (AEA Ad Hoc Committee on AI, if one exists) statement specifically on code/model-development disclosure; did not search for this directly as a distinct body and should be treated as an open gap.

## Q5: Overall honest risk assessment — is this disclosure (a) appropriately thorough/forward-leaning, (b) unusually extensive but not a problem, or (c) a potential red flag for reviewers/editors?

### Takeaway
My honest assessment, based on the above: this leans toward **(b) unusually extensive relative to current economics-journal norms, but defensible and probably not a fatal problem** — with a real but manageable risk of drifting toward (c) specifically because of the *combination and prominence* of all three disclosures (extensive named-agent code development + AI-assisted literature search + AI-assisted prose), rather than any single element in isolation. This is an inference synthesized from the cited findings above, not itself a separately sourced claim.

### Cited Findings (supporting the assessment)
- Every element of the disclosure (writing assistance named and bounded; code-development assistance with human direction and verification claimed; literature-search assistance with independent verification claimed; no AI authorship; author takes sole responsibility) tracks the *structure* that COPE, AEA, Econometric Society, Elsevier, Springer, and OUP all converge on: name the tool, describe the use, disclaim AI authorship, affirm human responsibility/verification — [COPE](https://publicationethics.org/guidance/cope-position/authorship-and-ai-tools); [AEA journal submissions]; [Springer Nature AI policy](https://www.springer.com/us/editorial-policies/artificial-intelligence--ai-/25428500).
- The level of *specificity* (naming "Claude Code" and "OpenAI's Codex" specifically, describing "~15,000 lines," describing "direct and continuous direction") exceeds what any economics-journal policy currently *requires* (AEA/Econometric Society ask only for a "brief description"; even OUP's and AFA/JF's stricter data/analysis language asks for tool+version+prompts, not necessarily this much narrative framing) — see Q2 findings.
- No published economics/public-finance journal article was found with a comparably detailed three-part disclosure (Q3 findings) — meaning the paper's author-note would likely be a genuine outlier in the Fiscal Studies submission pool, not something an editor has seen many times before.
- The under-1%-disclosure finding in bioethics scholarship ([PhilArchive bioethics study](https://philarchive.org/rec/BOBTMD)) suggests that the *modal* comparable paper simply doesn't disclose AI use in research production at all, meaning a reviewer's reference class for "normal" is mostly papers with zero disclosure or a one-line writing-only disclosure — making a long, specific, three-part disclosure stand out by contrast, independent of its actual content.

### Inferences
- **Why not (c) outright / a straightforward red flag**: Nothing in the disclosure violates any found policy — no AI authorship claimed, full human responsibility claimed, verification practices described for both code and literature, and the disclosure is in the right place (author-note/disclosure statement). Policy language from OUP, AFA/JF, and COPE anticipates exactly these categories (code, data/analysis, writing) as legitimately disclosable, so an editor applying the letter of current policy should find nothing actually *non-compliant* here — if anything, this disclosure is more compliant and more transparent than the field's norm, not less.
- **Why there is real risk of drawing scrutiny anyway**: Economics journal editors and referees, especially at a journal like Fiscal Studies that is unlikely to have seen many (if any) submissions with this scale of disclosed AI-code-development, may read the *specificity and prominence* of the disclosure as an implicit signal that AI contribution was large relative to the author's own — even though the stated framing (continuous direction, sole responsibility) is designed to rebut that inference. This is a perception/optics risk, not a policy-compliance risk. A referee's unstated worry is likely to be less "did you disclose properly" and more "if an AI agent wrote most of a 15,000-line codebase, how do I know the author understands/can defend every modeling choice in it," which is a question about the *author's command of their own model*, not about disclosure adequacy per se. The disclosure statement, however honestly and carefully written, cannot fully preempt that worry — it can only mitigate it.
- The literature-search disclosure (AI web search + independent verification of every citation against a primary source) is probably the *least* risky of the three on its own — it describes a workflow (AI-assisted search, human verification) that is explicitly endorsed as acceptable by every policy found (none ban AI-assisted search; all require only that the author verify/take responsibility for content, which this statement does).
- The prose-drafting disclosure is the *most* normalized of the three — squarely in line with COPE/AEA/Wiley/Elsevier/Springer boilerplate language, and least likely to draw any scrutiny at all.
- The code-development disclosure is the one most likely to draw editor/referee attention, not because it's non-compliant with any written policy, but because (i) it's the most novel practice (least precedent, per Q3-Q4), (ii) it's the one most tied to the paper's actual scientific content and reproducibility (a reviewer cares more about "is the model right" than "is the prose polished"), and (iii) "15,000 lines" developed "iteratively" by named coding agents is a striking, concrete number that invites a reviewer to mentally estimate what fraction of the intellectual work was delegated — an estimate the disclosure's own framing is trying to pre-empt but cannot fully control.
- Net honest judgment: this disclosure is very unlikely to constitute formal grounds for rejection or an ethics flag under any policy found. It is more likely to shape *referee tone* — possibly inviting extra scrutiny of the model's correctness, extra demands for code review/replication materials, or questions in review about which modeling decisions were the author's versus AI-suggested — than to be treated as a disclosure-adequacy problem. That is a real, non-trivial risk for a submission's reception, even though it is not a policy-violation risk.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence (editor statements, published editorial commentary, or a documented rejection/acceptance case) was found of how any economics editor has actually reacted to a disclosure of this type and scale. This assessment is therefore an informed inference from policy text and the absence of precedent, not a finding grounded in observed editorial behavior — this should be flagged clearly to the report reader as inference, not fact.
- Did not survey Fiscal Studies' own past issues for editorial tone/strictness on methodological transparency generally, which would sharpen this assessment for that specific venue.
