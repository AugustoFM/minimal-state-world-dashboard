# How Fiscal Studies Articles Structure Their Results Sections

Scope note on method: Fiscal Studies (Wiley/IFS) articles are almost entirely paywalled on Wiley Online Library (direct fetch attempts returned HTTP 403). To get real structural detail rather than abstracts alone, I located **near-identical working-paper versions** of three Fiscal Studies articles (the preprint/discussion-paper that the published article was directly drawn from) and read them in full. Two are close methodological twins of the published article (same authors, same model, same results, pre-journal-formatting); one (the COVID-Ireland paper) is a **later, extended working paper** by the same authors that incorporates and extends the analysis in the actual Fiscal Studies article — I flag this explicitly where it matters. I also identified five further Fiscal Studies articles by title/year/volume/issue through bibliographic search but did not obtain full text for them (listed in Gaps/supplementary identification below). I could not retrieve Wiley's own author-guidelines page (blocked) or confirm open-access badges directly on Wiley's site.

Papers examined in structural depth (all simulation/microsimulation, the closest comparables to a Monte Carlo fiscal model):

1. **Kornstad, T. and Thoresen, T.O. (2006), "Effects of Family Policy Reforms in Norway: Results from a Joint Labor Supply and Child Care Choice Microsimulation Analysis," *Fiscal Studies*, 27(3), 339–371** (DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8578.2006.00038.x). Read via Statistics Norway Discussion Paper No. 450 (March 2006), the pre-publication working-paper twin — https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp450.pdf. Discrete-choice microsimulation of four Norwegian family-policy reforms.
2. **Christl, M., De Poli, S. and Varga, J. (2022), "Reducing the Income Tax Burden for Households with Children: An Assessment of the Child Tax Credit Reform in Austria," *Fiscal Studies*, 43(2), 151–177** (DOI 10.1111/1475-5890.12295). Read via EUROMOD Working Paper EM 1/20 (Feb 2020), the pre-publication twin — https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/research/publications/working-papers/euromod/em1-20.pdf. Links EUROMOD microsimulation + discrete-choice labour supply model + QUEST DSGE macro model; closest comparable to a multi-stage Monte Carlo/behavioural fiscal model.
3. **O'Donoghue, C., Sologon, D.M., Kyzyma, I. and McHale, J. (2020), "Modelling the Distributional Impact of the COVID-19 Crisis," *Fiscal Studies*, 41(2), 321–336.** The Fiscal Studies article itself covered the first two waves. I read a related, longer working paper by the same authors extending the identical methodology to three waves — "A Microsimulation Analysis of the Distributional Impact over the Three Waves of the COVID-19 Crisis in Ireland" (2021), https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08398 — which cites the Fiscal Studies piece directly as its two-wave predecessor and reproduces its methodology/structure. **Caveat: this is not the published Fiscal Studies text itself**, so section-length proportions from it are indicative of this research group's house style, not confirmed exactly for the journal version.

## Do Fiscal Studies papers present a headline figure/table before or after methodology detail? Does the headline result appear before methodology?

### Takeaway
In all three papers examined, methodology is presented in full **before** any policy/treatment-effect results — there is no "headline result up front" pattern (no abstract-graphic, no results-first structure). Any figures that appear during the methods section are model-validation or context/motivation figures (e.g., goodness-of-fit, case counts), not the paper's headline findings.

### Cited Findings
- Kornstad & Thoresen (Fiscal Studies 2006 / SSB DP450) run Introduction → §2 motivating labour-market background (with Figure 1, a plain descriptive bar chart, not a result) → §3 decision-model methodology (with Table 1 classification scheme, Figure 2 model-fit check) → §4 welfare-measurement methodology → **only then** §5 "Effects of family policy reforms," which is where every policy-impact figure and number appears — [SSB DP450](https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp450.pdf).
- Christl, De Poli & Varga (Fiscal Studies 2022 / EUROMOD WP 1/20) run §1 Introduction → §2 Literature review → §3 description of the reform itself (with a hypothetical-household figure, Figure 3, illustrating the policy design, not an empirical result) → §4 "Data and Methodology" (EUROMOD, labour-supply model, QUEST, and how they are combined) → **only then** §5–§8 contain all results (fiscal/distributional, labour-market, macro, second-round) — [EUROMOD EM1/20](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/research/publications/working-papers/euromod/em1-20.pdf).
- O'Donoghue et al.'s COVID microsimulation paper (extended working-paper version of Fiscal Studies 41(2)) runs §1 Introduction → §2 Methodology (nowcasting approach) → §3 "Data and simulation assumptions" (seven tables of policy-parameter detail, e.g. benefit-rate schedules, commuting-cost estimates) → **only then** §4 "Results" → §5 Discussion and conclusions — [arXiv:2103.08398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08398).
- In all three, the paper's actual headline quantitative claims (e.g., "labour supply increases by about 13%... approximately 12,000 man-years" in Kornstad & Thoresen; "total employment increases by about 0.17 percent after 5 years" in Christl et al.; the Gini/redistribution trend in O'Donoghue et al.) appear only in the dedicated results section(s), never in the introduction as a lead figure, though the introduction does state the qualitative direction of the main finding in prose (e.g. Christl et al.'s abstract/intro state "the reform reduces inequality, lowers the poverty rate... positive impact on labour supply, especially for women" before any numbers are given).

### Inferences
- The convention in this journal (at least among simulation-based empirical papers) is methodology-first, consistent with standard applied-economics journal norms rather than a "results-forward" style sometimes seen in policy-brief or medical-journal formats. A paper's "headline" is communicated qualitatively in the abstract/introduction, but the actual numbers and figures are reserved for the results section after methods are fully laid out.
- For a Monte Carlo debt-sustainability paper, this suggests it is standard/expected to fully specify the stochastic model and data before presenting any percentile-band projection figures — i.e., do not open with the fan chart before explaining how it was built.

### Gaps
- I did not find a Fiscal Studies article that leads with a results figure/graphical abstract before methods; it's possible some exist (e.g., shorter "policy note" format pieces the journal also publishes), but none turned up in search or in the papers examined.

## How much quantitative material lives in the main text vs. appendices? Is there a "one headline figure + one headline table in text, detailed sensitivity tables in appendix" pattern?

### Takeaway
Yes, directionally: all three papers push **full disaggregated breakdowns** (by decile/sector/skill level/time-point, or underlying regression/estimation tables) to lettered/numbered appendices, while the main text carries one or two illustrative headline figures plus a small number of aggregate/summary tables. None of the three, however, uses a single "one figure + one table" minimalism — the main text in each case still carries multiple (4–10) tables and figures; the appendix is reserved specifically for *granular* or *supporting-estimation* material, not for a secondary "headline" result.

### Cited Findings
- Kornstad & Thoresen: main text carries Figures 1–7 and Tables 1–2 (the final Table 2, "Summary of effects of various family policy reforms," is the single cross-reform comparison table and appears in the concluding §6 "Summary," not earlier). The genuinely technical material — the full discrete-choice model derivation, parameter table (Table A1-1), and the formal compensating-variation (CV) derivation — is pushed to **Appendix 1** ("A more detailed exposition of the decision model") and **Appendix 2** ("Calculating compensating variation"). The main-text results discussion explicitly defers one piece of a specific result to the appendix: "the distribution of CV in Figure A4-1 is rather similar to the description of CV in Figure 4" — [SSB DP450, pp. 20–21, 31–37](https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp450.pdf).
- Christl, De Poli & Varga: main text (§5–§8) carries roughly 13 tables/figures of aggregate results (fiscal impact Table 2, inequality Table 3, poverty Table 4, implicit-tax-rate Tables 5–6, elasticity Figures 6–8, labour-supply-response Figures 9–11, macro impulse-response Figures 12–13, dynamic-scoring Tables 8–10). The **Appendix** holds purely disaggregated/supporting material: decile-by-decile ITR changes (Figure 14), gender/household-type breakdowns of an already-shown aggregate result (Figure 15), skill-level breakdowns of employment/wage impulse responses already shown in aggregate in the main text (Figures 16–18), the government-budget-balance impulse response (Figure 19), and the full Heckman wage-equation estimation table (Table 11) — [EUROMOD EM1/20, pp. 34–39](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/research/publications/working-papers/euromod/em1-20.pdf).
- O'Donoghue et al. (extended COVID working paper): main text §4 "Results" carries exactly one figure (Figure 3, a three-panel distributional chart: market/disposable/adjusted-disposable income by decile) and three tables (Table 8 average incomes, Table 9 Gini coefficients, Table 10 redistribution decomposition) — a genuinely minimal "headline figure + a few summary tables" pattern. All of the underlying calibration detail (commuting-cost regression, Table B.1; childcare regression, Table C.1; share-holding IPF tables, Tables D.1–D.2) and, notably, the **full decile-by-time-point breakdown of every income measure** (Table E.1, which is essentially the disaggregated version of headline Figure 3/Table 8) are relegated to Appendices A–E — [arXiv:2103.08398, pp. 14–18, 22–25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08398).

### Inferences
- The clearest and most portable pattern across all three: **the full cross-tabulated/disaggregated version of a result that is shown in the text only in summary/aggregate form goes to the appendix.** E.g., O'Donoghue shows one summary decile chart in-text (Figure 3) and the full numeric decile-by-wave table (Table E.1) in the appendix; Christl shows an aggregate ITR change by decile in-text narratively but the full statistical chart with confidence intervals in the appendix (Figure 14); skill-level or sub-group disaggregation of an already-reported aggregate macro effect goes to the appendix (Christl, Figures 16–18).
- For a stochastic debt-sustainability paper with percentile bands under multiple policy scenarios, the analogous move would be: main text carries one fan-chart figure per key scenario plus a small number of summary statistic tables (e.g., median/P10/P90 debt ratio in target year, probability of debt stabilising); the full per-year, per-percentile numeric table and any disaggregation (e.g., by state/region, by shock type) goes to an appendix.
- None of the three papers' main texts is as spare as "one figure + one table total" — Fiscal Studies simulation papers evidently tolerate a fairly table/figure-dense main text (8–13 display items is typical), with the appendix doing the job of hiding *granularity*, not hiding *entire secondary analyses*.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether Fiscal Studies has a formal "Supporting Information" / online-only appendix policy (as many Wiley journals do) distinct from an in-PDF appendix — none of the three papers I accessed were the final typeset Wiley PDF, so I cannot confirm whether any appendix material was moved to a web-only supplement at the publication stage. This is worth checking directly against a live Wiley PDF if you can get institutional access.
- I was not able to load Wiley's "Author Guidelines" page for Fiscal Studies (blocked with HTTP 403) to check for an explicit policy on word/page limits or appendix placement.

## How many results subsections do typical simulation papers have, and what are they usually called?

### Takeaway
Results subsections are typically organised **thematically around the type of outcome or the stage of the analysis**, not generically labelled "Results." Counts range from 2 (one results section with 2 subsections) to 4 full, separately numbered results sections (each with its own subsections) in the more elaborate multi-model paper.

### Cited Findings
- Kornstad & Thoresen: a single numbered results section, "5. Effects of family policy reforms," internally divided by *policy scenario* rather than by outcome type: "Base-line system," "Increasing the number of spaces at child care centers, the abolition of queues," "Rate cuts," "Rescinding the home care allowance," "A modified Working Families' Tax Credit system." A separate prior section, "4. Measures of distributional effects," is methodological (defines CV, income-inequality framework, social ranking) rather than results-bearing. The paper closes with "6. Summary," which contains the one cross-cutting comparison table — [SSB DP450](https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp450.pdf).
- Christl, De Poli & Varga: **four** separately numbered results sections, each organised by *type of effect*: "5. The fiscal and distributional impact of the reform" (5.1 "The fiscal impact," 5.2 "The distributional impact"); "6. Labour market and work incentive impact of the 2019 reform" (6.1 "Implicit tax rates on labour," 6.2 "Labour supply responses"); "7. Macroeconomic impact of the reform" (no further subsections, organised narratively by variable: employment, wages, then GDP/consumption/investment); "8. 2nd round effects at micro-level" (dynamic-scoring reconciliation back to the household level) — [EUROMOD EM1/20](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/research/publications/working-papers/euromod/em1-20.pdf).
- O'Donoghue et al.: one numbered results section, "4. Results," with exactly two named subsections: "Average and Distributional Analysis" and "Inequality and Redistribution" — [arXiv:2103.08398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08398).
- None of the three papers uses the generic subsection labels "Baseline results," "Sensitivity analysis," or "Robustness checks" that are common in other applied-economics journals. The closest functional analogue to a robustness/extension section is Christl et al.'s "2nd round effects at micro-level" (§8), which re-runs the distributional/poverty analysis after feeding macro (QUEST) output back into the microsimulation — effectively a dynamic-scoring robustness exercise, but titled descriptively by what it does, not generically labelled "Robustness."

### Inferences
- The house style favours **content-descriptive subsection titles** (naming the outcome variable, the policy scenario, or the analytical step) over generic templated headers. A debt-sustainability paper modelled on this convention would likely do better with subsections like "Baseline projection," "Fan chart under the fiscal rule scenario," "Sensitivity to the interest-rate–growth differential" rather than a generic "Robustness checks" header — though the latter is not forbidden, it's simply not what these examples do.
- Complexity of the model (number of linked sub-models) appears to track directly with the number of distinct results sections: Kornstad & Thoresen (one microsimulation model) → 1 results section with scenario subsections; Christl et al. (three linked models: microsimulation + labour supply + CGE) → 4 distinct results sections, one per "stage"/model in the pipeline. For a paper that itself has multiple analytical layers (baseline Monte Carlo projection, policy scenarios, and separate robustness/diagnostic checks), a Christl-et-al.-style multi-section split (e.g., "Baseline stochastic projection," "Policy scenario comparison," "Robustness and diagnostic checks") is the better-supported analogy.

### Gaps
- Sample size is small (3 papers with full text); I cannot quantify a modal "typical" count with confidence across the broader journal. The bibliographically-identified-but-unread papers (Blundell/Duncan/McCrae/Meghir 2000; Blundell/Bozio/Laroque 2013; Brewer/Browne/Jin 2012; Hérault/Kalb 2022) would be useful to examine for a larger sample, but I did not obtain full text for them.

## Numbered propositions, bullet-point takeaways, or prose-only presentation?

### Takeaway
All three papers are **prose-only** in their results sections — no numbered propositions/findings, no bullet-point "key takeaways." Every statistic is embedded in narrative sentences that cite the specific table or figure number. Bullet points do appear elsewhere in these papers, but only in **methodological/descriptive** passages (listing channels of a shock, listing model components), never to summarise results.

### Cited Findings
- Kornstad & Thoresen's results narrative is a continuous sequence of paragraphs like: "In total, expected hours of work for this group of females increases by approximately 4 percent, or about one hour per week on average... a growth of about 3,500 man-years," with every figure/table referenced inline (e.g., "cf. Figure 4") — no bullets or numbered findings anywhere in §5 — [SSB DP450, pp. 16–27](https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp450.pdf).
- Christl, De Poli & Varga use bullet points exactly once in the entire paper, and it is in the methodology section listing how work incentives can be split ("extensive margin... intensive margin"), not in the results. All quantitative findings in §5–§8 are delivered in prose paragraphs, e.g., "the income tax revenues are reduced by 1.55 billion Euro (or 4.7 percent)... only about 38 Millions are due to the 'Kindermehrbetrag'" — [EUROMOD EM1/20](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/research/publications/working-papers/euromod/em1-20.pdf).
- O'Donoghue et al. use bullet points in their §2 Methodology to enumerate the channels through which COVID-19 affects household income (six bullets, e.g., "Those who get sick have a spectrum of consequences..."), but §4 "Results" itself is pure prose, paragraph by paragraph against each table — [arXiv:2103.08398, pp. 4–5, 14–18](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08398).

### Inferences
- Bullet points, where used at all in this journal's simulation papers, serve to enumerate *mechanisms, assumptions, or model components* in the methodology — not to summarise empirical findings. A results section modelled on this convention should present percentile-band/scenario findings in full prose paragraphs tied explicitly to figure/table numbers, reserving any bullet list for describing, e.g., the channels through which a shock or policy affects the debt path, in the methods section.
- Despite the complete absence of numbered propositions, each paper still ends its empirical narrative with an explicit, compact comparison device: Kornstad & Thoresen's closing cross-reform Table 2; Christl et al.'s closing dynamic-scoring tables (Tables 8–10) immediately followed by a conclusion section that restates, in prose, the three or four main quantitative takeaways. This suggests the "takeaway" function bullets might otherwise serve is instead handled by (a) a compact summary table late in the results and (b) a prose-restated set of headline numbers in the conclusion.

### Gaps
- Cannot confirm whether this prose-only convention is a hard journal norm (e.g., enforced by editors/style guide) or simply common practice among the economists who publish there; I found no explicit Fiscal Studies style guidance on this point.

## How long are typical results sections relative to the whole paper?

### Takeaway
Based on the three full papers read (noting these are working-paper/preprint versions, generally longer than the trimmed, typeset journal version), results sections occupy roughly a **third to a half** of total page count, with methodology/data typically occupying a comparable or somewhat larger share; introduction and conclusion together are usually under a fifth of the paper.

### Cited Findings
- Kornstad & Thoresen (SSB DP450, 37 pages of content before references, excluding appendices): Introduction ~2 pages (5%); scope/motivation §2 ~2 pages; methodology §§3–4 ~10 pages (27%); results §5 ~10 pages (27%); summary/conclusion §6 ~2 pages (5%); references + two technical appendices ~11 pages (30%, but this is pure mathematical derivation, not sensitivity analysis) — [SSB DP450](https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp450.pdf).
- Christl, De Poli & Varga (EUROMOD EM1/20, 33 pages before references/appendix): Introduction + literature review ~4 pages (12%); reform description + methodology (§§3–4) ~9 pages (27%); the four results sections (§§5–8) combined ~16 pages (48%); conclusion ~1.5 pages (4%); this is the most results-heavy of the three, consistent with it having the most elaborate multi-model pipeline to report on — [EUROMOD EM1/20](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/research/publications/working-papers/euromod/em1-20.pdf).
- O'Donoghue et al. (arXiv:2103.08398, 21 pages before references/appendices): Introduction ~3 pages (14%); methodology §2 ~3 pages (14%); data/assumptions §3 ~8 pages (38%, unusually large because of the many policy-parameter tables needed to document a fast-moving, frequently-changing set of COVID benefit schedules); results §4 ~5 pages (24%); discussion/conclusion ~1.5 pages (7%) — [arXiv:2103.08398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08398).

### Inferences
- There is real variation (24%–48% of main text) depending on how many distinct analytical stages the paper reports on and how much institutional/calibration detail the policy environment requires (O'Donoghue's COVID paper needed unusually long "data and assumptions" documentation because benefit rates changed repeatedly within the sample period — a parallel exists for a paper documenting a changing/complex set of policy-scenario assumptions for Brazil).
- A reasonable planning benchmark for a Fiscal Studies submission: results (all results subsections combined) should be the single largest block of the paper, in the ~30–50% range of main-text pages, methodology/data a comparable or slightly smaller block, and intro+conclusion together under ~20%.

### Gaps
- All three source documents are working-paper versions, typically run longer than the final journal article (the journal enforces page/word limits Wiley does not disclose in material I could access). The proportions above should be read as indicative of internal balance, not as a prediction of absolute journal page counts. I was not able to confirm Fiscal Studies' actual word/page limit or typeset page count conventions directly.
