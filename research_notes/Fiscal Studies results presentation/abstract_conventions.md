# How Fiscal Studies Abstracts and Introductions Present Quantitative Findings

Scope note: this covers only the abstract/introduction conventions of the journal *Fiscal Studies* (Wiley-Blackwell, for the Institute for Fiscal Studies). Primary sources are full-text article pages read directly on Wiley Online Library (onlinelibrary.wiley.com/journal/14755890), not search snippets, except where explicitly marked as a secondary/unverified source. Eight articles were read in full (abstract + introduction, several in full); all quotes below are verbatim from those pages.

Articles examined directly (full abstract + introduction read verbatim from Wiley):
1. Sutherland, Hancock, Hills & Zantomio, "Keeping up or Falling behind? The Impact of Benefit and Tax Uprating on Incomes and Poverty," *Fiscal Studies* 29(4), 2008, pp. 467–498. DOI 10.1111/j.1475-5890.2008.00082.x.
2. Figari, Paulus, Sutherland, Tsakloglou, Verbist & Zantomio, "Removing Homeownership Bias in Taxation: The Distributional Effects of Including Net Imputed Rent in Taxable Income," *Fiscal Studies* 38(4), 2017, pp. 525–557. DOI 10.1111/1475-5890.12105.
3. O'Donoghue, Sologon, Kyzyma & McHale, "Modelling the Distributional Impact of the COVID-19 Crisis," *Fiscal Studies* 41(2), 2020, pp. 321–336. DOI 10.1111/1475-5890.12231.
4. Besley & Stern, "The Economics of Lockdown," *Fiscal Studies* 41(3) (COVID-19 Economic Crisis special issue), 2020, pp. 493–513. DOI 10.1111/1475-5890.12246.
5. Moffitt & Ziliak, "COVID-19 and the US Safety Net," *Fiscal Studies* 41(3), 2020, pp. 515–548. DOI 10.1111/1475-5890.12243.
6. Hupkau & Petrongolo, "Work, Care and Gender during the COVID-19 Crisis," *Fiscal Studies* 41(3), 2020, pp. 623–651. DOI 10.1111/1475-5890.12245.
7. Advani, "Who does and doesn't pay taxes?," *Fiscal Studies* 43(1), 2022, pp. 5–22. DOI 10.1111/1475-5890.12257.
8. Onnis, Piga, Conti & Bottasso, "VAT cuts as emergency policy intervention: evidence from the UK case," *Fiscal Studies*, 2026 (in press, Vol 47 Issue 3). DOI 10.1111/1475-5890.70033.

Note on access: both ifs.org.uk and a direct automated WebFetch tool returned HTTP 403 on every attempt (bot-blocked). All verbatim text below was instead obtained by driving a real Chrome browser session to the Wiley DOI pages, which rendered normally and is the basis for every quote in this file.

## Do Fiscal Studies abstracts state specific headline numbers, or do they stay qualitative/directional? What's the typical mix?

### Takeaway
Across the 8 abstracts read in full, the majority (5 of 8) contain zero specific numbers for their headline finding, preferring qualitative/directional language ("near doubling," "substantial gain," "equalizing effect," "roughly equally affected," "inadequate response") even when the underlying paper is a heavily quantitative microsimulation or regression study. The 3 abstracts that do give numbers favor round, hedged figures (a range, a fraction, or "about X per cent") rather than precise decimals — none used a number like "23.4 percentage points" in the abstract itself.

### Cited Findings
- Sutherland, Hancock, Hills & Zantomio (2008) abstract gives zero precise statistics; its headline claim is phrased as "a near doubling of the child poverty rate alongside a substantial gain to the public finances" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-5890.2008.00082.x)
- O'Donoghue, Sologon, Kyzyma & McHale (2020) abstract, despite being a dynamic calibrated microsimulation paper, states only: "Overall, we find that the crisis had an equalizing real-time effect for both gross and disposable incomes, notwithstanding the significant hardship experienced by many households" — no numbers at all — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12231)
- Besley & Stern (2020), a conceptual/framework paper, has a purely qualitative, number-free abstract: "In this paper, we suggest an approach to analysing policies relating to the COVID-19 pandemic. We discuss the formulation of policy and sketch how the approach can be applied to different specific challenges..." — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12246)
- Moffitt & Ziliak (2020) abstract is also number-free even though the paper's introduction is packed with exact statistics (see Q3): "...an inadequate response compared with the magnitude of the downturn" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12243)
- Hupkau & Petrongolo (2020) abstract, from a paper full of regression tables, stays qualitative: "labour market outcomes of men and women were roughly equally affected at the extensive margin... women suffered smaller losses at the intensive margin, experiencing slightly smaller changes in hours and earnings... women provided on average a larger share of increased childcare needs" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12245)
- Figari et al. (2017) abstract uses one soft quantifier, not a number: "The results show how including net imputed rent in the tax base might affect inequality... Housing taxation appears to be a promising avenue..." The one line closest to a headline magnitude, in the introduction rather than abstract, is still qualitative: "a small inequality-reducing effect of net imputed rent taxation, consistent across countries" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12105)
- Onnis, Piga, Conti & Bottasso (2026) is the clearest counterexample — its abstract gives a specific numeric range: "We find a statistically significant contemporaneous pass-through to hotel room prices that varies between around 20 and 50 per cent, with a peak effect on prices observed in the second week after the reform" — but immediately qualifies it: "However, the pass-through effect is the outcome of a discretionary approach as discounts were negligible for rooms with check-in dates two months after the policy introduction" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.70033)
- Advani (2022) abstract gives rounded fractions: "Non-compliance is common, with one-third of taxpayers underpaying by some amount, although half of aggregate under-reporting is done by just 2 per cent of taxpayers" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12257)

### Inferences
- The split roughly tracks paper type: simulation/distributional papers in this sample (Sutherland, O'Donoghue, Figari, Moffitt-Ziliak distributional claims, Hupkau-Petrongolo) lean qualitative in the abstract even when the paper itself is saturated with exact figures; causal/policy-evaluation papers with one or two cleanly estimated parameters (VAT pass-through, tax non-compliance share) are more willing to put a rounded number or range in the abstract.
- Even the "numeric" abstracts avoid false precision: a range ("20 and 50 per cent") or a round fraction ("one-third," "2 per cent") rather than a single decimal estimate. This suggests a house style that treats the abstract as a plain-language, hedged headline rather than a results-table excerpt.
- For a paper whose central quantitative claim is itself fragile or context-dependent (varies by scenario/country/ambition, as in the user's own debt/Gini-reversal paper), the closest analogues in this sample (Sutherland's "near doubling... alongside a substantial gain," Onnis's "20 and 50 per cent... however...") suggest the convention is to state the direction and rough order of magnitude, then immediately flag the caveat/heterogeneity in the same sentence or the next one, rather than commit to a single point estimate.

### Gaps
- Sample size is 8 articles, not a journal-wide census; a systematic count across, say, 50+ issues was not performed within the available tool budget, so the "5 qualitative / 3 numeric" split should be read as indicative of a strong tendency, not a precise journal-wide ratio.
- No pure debt-to-GDP/public-finances simulation or projection paper from *Fiscal Studies* itself was successfully located and read (several search attempts for "debt sustainability," "debt projection" Fiscal Studies articles returned only non-Fiscal-Studies sources — IMF, World Bank, CPB, NZ Treasury). This is the single topic closest to the user's own paper's headline claim (debt/GDP rising 12–23 points) and could not be directly benchmarked.

## How long are typical Fiscal Studies abstracts (word count), and how many distinct findings do they usually pack in?

### Takeaway
Abstract length varied widely across the 8 articles, from about 40 words (a short conceptual paper) to about 190 words (a multi-country, multi-scenario microsimulation paper); most cluster in the 80–120-word range. Typical abstracts state one to three distinct findings/points, not more — even multi-scenario papers compress several simulation results into one or two summary sentences rather than enumerating each result.

### Cited Findings
- Besley & Stern (2020): abstract is 2 sentences, approx. 40 words, stating an approach/contribution rather than a finding (0 distinct empirical findings; this is explicitly a framework-setting paper) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12246)
- Sutherland et al. (2008): approx. 100–110 words, packing 2 headline findings (child poverty near-doubles; large fiscal gain) plus one qualifying point (pensioners protected) plus a closing normative statement about UK child poverty targets — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-5890.2008.00082.x)
- O'Donoghue et al. (2020): approx. 90 words, 1 headline finding (equalizing real-time effect) stated once, with a hedge clause — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12231)
- Moffitt & Ziliak (2020): approx. 85 words, 1 headline finding (inadequate safety-net response) plus a policy-recommendation sentence — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12243)
- Hupkau & Petrongolo (2020): approx. 110 words, 3 distinct findings (extensive-margin parity; intensive-margin asymmetry favoring women; unequal within-household childcare split) plus one closing interpretive sentence — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12245)
- Advani (2022): approx. 80 words, 3 distinct quantitative findings (prevalence of non-compliance, concentration among 2% of taxpayers, variation by third-party reporting/industry/demographics) stated in rapid succession — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12257)
- Onnis et al. (2026): approx. 100 words, 2 findings (pass-through magnitude/timing; discretionary incompleteness for later bookings) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.70033)
- Figari et al. (2017): approx. 190 words (the longest in this sample), describing the research question, 6-country method, 3 policy scenarios, and a 2-part headline finding (first-order distributional effects; "promising avenue" framing). This paper additionally carries a separate, unique "Policy points" box of 4 bullet points placed right after the abstract — not part of the abstract word count, but a related structural feature — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12105)

### Inferences
- Length scales with design complexity (number of countries/scenarios) more than with the number of findings reported in prose — Figari et al.'s 190-word abstract still boils its six-country, three-scenario simulation down to roughly 2 headline sentences, pushing the scenario-by-scenario detail into a separate "Policy points" box and the results section rather than enumerating it in the abstract itself.
- The "Policy points" box (seen on the Figari et al. article, labeled explicitly "Policy points" with 4 short bullets restating the paper's practical implications in plain language) appears to be an optional/era-specific Fiscal Studies feature for some papers, distinct from the abstract — worth checking in the submission guidelines, since it is a second, even-more-compressed summary vehicle that might be a better home for a crisp "debt rises 12–23 points" style bullet than the abstract itself.
- None of the 8 abstracts read like a results-table dump; even the information-dense ones resolve to roughly 1–3 sentence-level "findings," suggesting that cramming the paper's full set of simulation permutations into the abstract would be stylistically atypical for this journal.

### Gaps
- Whether the "Policy points" feature is universal, optional, or tied to a specific article type/era could not be confirmed from the pages read (it appeared on the Figari et al. 2017 article but not on the Wiley article pages for several other articles checked, e.g. Sutherland et al. 2008 predates it and Besley & Stern 2020 does not have one) — this should be checked against current *Fiscal Studies* author guidelines before relying on it.
- Precise word counts are estimated by eye from the rendered text, not run through a word-counting tool; treat the figures above as approximate (±10–15 words).

## Do introductions restate or expand on the abstract's headline numbers, or hold back quantitative detail for the results section?

### Takeaway
Introductions consistently go beyond the abstract in quantitative specificity — but in two different ways: (a) some papers keep the abstract qualitative and then load the final "preview of results" paragraph(s) of the introduction with many exact figures that never appear in the abstract at all (Moffitt & Ziliak is the clearest case), while (b) others restate the same headline claim with just one added number or a slightly sharper range (Onnis et al.). Precise multi-country or multi-scenario breakdowns (e.g., exact Gini-coefficient changes) are still reserved for results tables even in the introduction.

### Cited Findings
- Moffitt & Ziliak (2020): abstract is fully qualitative (see Q1), but the introduction's results-preview paragraph states exact figures nowhere in the abstract: "90–10 income inequality increased 22 per cent in the six years after the Great Recession... this inequality retrenched by about 10 per cent... the US tax system reduces before tax inequality a robust 45 per cent in a typical year" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12243)
- Onnis et al. (2026): the introduction's "Our results can be summarised as follows" paragraph restates the abstract's 20–50 per cent range with added texture: "we estimate a robust and statistically significant pass-through of the VAT cut, ranging from roughly 20 to 50 per cent... the peak effect occurs in the second week after the reform" — an expansion, not a new number, but sharper framing of timing — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.70033)
- Figari et al. (2017): introduction restates the same qualitative framing as the abstract ("The results provide evidence of a small inequality-reducing effect of net imputed rent taxation, consistent across countries") without giving the actual Gini-point changes; those (e.g., Belgium Gini falls 2.1%, Netherlands falls 2.6% under scenario IR1; Netherlands falls 4.8% under IR3) appear only in Table 6 of the results section — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12105)
- Advani (2022): introduction closely mirrors the abstract's two headline numbers (one-third underpaying; 2% responsible for half of under-reporting) without adding much beyond them in the "We show three main results" paragraph, but Section 2 ("UK Tax Gap"), reached only after the roadmap, introduces many new figures (£35 billion aggregate gap, 5.6% of liabilities, US gap 16.4%, Canada 10.6–12.6%) that were absent from both abstract and introduction's results-preview — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12257)
- Besley & Stern (2020): the abstract has zero numbers, but the introduction immediately supplies historical/contextual (not result) numbers absent from the abstract: state spending "rising from around 10 to 40 per cent of GDP over the [20th] century," the Black Death "around 50 million" deaths, and "around 9 million workers" on the UK furlough scheme — showing that even a number-free abstract is often followed by a numerically rich scene-setting introduction, albeit for context rather than for the paper's own result — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12246)

### Inferences
- The introduction functions as the real "quantitative headline," not the abstract, in at least several of these papers — a reader who wants the magnitude has to read past the abstract into the last paragraph(s) of Section I. This is a notable convention: a quantitatively modest abstract does not imply a quantitatively modest paper.
- Even when the introduction does carry exact figures, there is a second deferral step: country/scenario-by-scenario breakdowns and precise distributional-index values (Gini, Atkinson) are consistently held back for the results section tables, even from the introduction. Only the paper's single most defensible aggregate number tends to surface before the results section.
- For the user's own paper, this suggests a viable structure: an abstract that states the debt/GDP range and the direction of the Gini finding in hedged, qualitative-leaning terms (as Sutherland et al. and Onnis et al. do), followed by an introduction closing paragraph that previews the specific percentage-point range(s) and flags the diagnostic reversal — while holding the full scenario-by-scenario and interest-income-channel decomposition for the results section.

### Gaps
- Only one-directional evidence was gathered (introductions adding to abstracts); whether any Fiscal Studies introduction ever contains fewer numbers than its own abstract was not observed in this sample but also was not specifically searched for.

## Is there a common "motivation" paragraph structure (gap in literature → this paper's contribution → headline finding → roadmap) for papers that respond to or extend prior work/criticism?

### Takeaway
Yes — this exact four-part structure (contextualize the literature → explicitly name a gap, sometimes naming the one prior paper that is the partial exception → state the paper's contribution in a first-person "In this paper, we..." sentence → preview the headline finding → close with an explicit section-by-section roadmap) appears consistently across the non-conceptual papers in this sample. No formal "Comment on..." / "Reply to..." article pair from *Fiscal Studies* was directly verified, so the "responds to criticism" framing is inferred from papers that position themselves against a literature gap or open debate rather than against a named rebuttal.

### Cited Findings
- Figari et al. (2017) is the clearest textbook case: para. 1–2 establish the phenomenon and cite foundational literature (Aaron 1970; Rosen 1979; Poterba 1992); the gap is named explicitly with one acknowledged exception — "Although a removal of the homeownership bias is generally advocated on efficiency grounds, its distributional implications are often neglected, especially in Europe, and in a cross-country perspective... with the exception of Matsaganis and Flevotomou (2007a), who quantify the distributional impact of mortgage interest tax relief in five European countries"; the contribution statement follows directly — "The aim of this paper is to contribute to this literature by investigating the first-order effects..."; the headline finding is given at the end of the introduction ("The results provide evidence of a small inequality-reducing effect..."); and the paper closes section I with an explicit roadmap, "The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. In Section II..." — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12105)
- Advani (2022) follows the same skeleton: motivation (scale of the UK tax gap), gap/contribution ("A key input for this research is a clear measure of where in the income distribution under-reporting is taking place... In this paper, we use data from random tax audits in the UK to study who is under-reporting income... We show three main results. First... Second... Finally..."), then an explicit roadmap: "The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 provides some context... Section 5 concludes" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12257)
- Onnis et al. (2026) frames its contribution against an open empirical debate rather than a specific rival paper: "The effectiveness of these emergency and targeted interventions remains a particularly contentious issue and there is still much uncertainty about their empirical effects (e.g. Crossley, Low and Sleeman, 2014; Benzarti, Garriga and Tortarolo, 2024; Bernardino et al., 2025)... Our contribution is to extend this evidence to hotel accommodation, where pass-through is highly policy-relevant but much less directly documented," followed by "Our results can be summarised as follows..." and then "The paper is organised as follows. In Section 2..." — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.70033)
- Moffitt & Ziliak (2020) uses a close variant: motivation (the pandemic "laid bare the many holes" in the US safety net), contribution ("In this paper, we use two decades of data to examine trends..."), then a multi-paragraph results preview that doubles as the roadmap (each paragraph previews one section's findings) rather than a single terminal "roadmap" sentence — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12243)
- Sutherland et al. (2008) opens by directly motivating from a policy/political gap rather than a literature gap: "In the UK, the basis for these upratings is rarely debated, yet has major long-term consequences..." — i.e., the "gap" being filled is a policy-debate gap, not strictly an academic-literature gap, which is itself a notable alternative framing device — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-5890.2008.00082.x)

### Inferences
- The four-part structure (context → gap → contribution → finding-preview → roadmap) is a load-bearing convention across empirical *Fiscal Studies* papers of very different subfields (tax microsimulation, tax compliance, VAT pass-through), which suggests it would read as "in-house style" if the user's paper follows it too — including explicitly naming the prior work/claim being revisited (the "omitted interest-income-distribution channel raised by an independent reviewer") the same way Figari et al. name Matsaganis and Flevotomou (2007a) as the one partial precedent, and the way Onnis et al. name three specific prior papers that leave the question open.
- Papers that are explicitly about extending/correcting a result (as opposed to opening a brand-new question) still use "contentious issue... much uncertainty about... empirical effects" (Onnis et al.) or "often neglected... with the exception of" (Figari et al.) as the gap-naming device, rather than a blunter "X claimed Y, but we show Z" framing. This suggests the user's paper should likewise motivate the Gini-reversal finding as resolving an acknowledged gap/uncertainty (the omitted channel) rather than as a direct rebuttal, matching the journal's measured tone (also visible in Besley & Stern's hedged, cautious register throughout).

### Gaps
- No formal "Comment on [Article]" or "Reply" article type was located and verified on Wiley for *Fiscal Studies* within the available search budget (one secondary, unverified search snippet mentioned a 1999 Haufler article "with a companion comment by Michael P. Devereux," but this was not opened and confirmed from the primary source, so it should not be relied on without independent verification).
- Whether Fiscal Studies has ever published a paper explicitly structured as "reconsidering our own earlier finding in light of an omitted channel" (the precise situation facing the user's paper) was not found in this sample; the closest analogues found are papers extending an open debate (Onnis et al.) or filling a literature gap with one named partial-exception paper (Figari et al.), not papers revising a specific prior finding's sign.

## Are there examples of abstracts explicitly flagging a surprising, counterintuitive, or sign-reversing finding, and how is that phrased?

### Takeaway
None of the 8 abstracts read in full uses explicit "surprising," "counterintuitive," or "contrary to expectations" language. The closest devices found are (a) juxtaposing two findings that pull in opposite directions within the same sentence using a neutral connector ("alongside," "notwithstanding," "however," "but"), and (b) stating a positive headline result immediately followed by a one-sentence qualifier that undercuts or bounds it. The journal's house tone in this sample is measured and hedged rather than dramatized, even when the underlying finding is counterintuitive.

### Cited Findings
- Sutherland et al. (2008) juxtaposes two opposite-direction consequences in one sentence without surprise-language: "Continuing these policies for 20 years, other things staying the same, would result in a near doubling of the child poverty rate alongside a substantial gain to the public finances" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-5890.2008.00082.x)
- O'Donoghue et al. (2020) uses "notwithstanding" as the contrast connector for its most counterintuitive-sounding claim (a crisis producing an "equalizing" effect): "Overall, we find that the crisis had an equalizing real-time effect for both gross and disposable incomes, notwithstanding the significant hardship experienced by many households" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12231)
- Onnis et al. (2026) uses "However" to flag the qualifying, less-expected part of its finding (incomplete, discretionary pass-through) immediately after stating the main positive result: "We find a statistically significant contemporaneous pass-through to hotel room prices that varies between around 20 and 50 per cent... However, the pass-through effect is the outcome of a discretionary approach..." — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.70033)
- Hupkau & Petrongolo (2020) flags a result that runs against a prevailing (US-based) expectation of worse outcomes for women, using "if anything" as the hedge rather than "surprisingly": "labour market outcomes of men and women were roughly equally affected at the extensive margin... But, if anything, women suffered smaller losses at the intensive margin" — and the body text explicitly contrasts this with the opposite finding from a comparable US study (Alon et al. 2020) and a UK real-time survey (Adams-Prassl et al. 2020), stating "This result is in contrast with evidence for the US..." and "It also somewhat differs from evidence based on real-time data for the United Kingdom..." — but this contrast-with-prior-literature framing appears only in the body/results section, not the abstract — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12245)

### Inferences
- The absence of "surprising"/"counterintuitive" framing even where the result plausibly warrants it (an equalizing pandemic effect; a VAT cut that is only partially and asymmetrically passed through) suggests this is a deliberate house-style choice for *Fiscal Studies* abstracts: let the juxtaposition of facts imply the tension, rather than naming it as a surprise. For the user's paper, this suggests phrasing the sign-reversal finding as a plain juxtaposition — e.g., "a small reduction in the Gini coefficient... which reverses sign once [channel] is incorporated" — rather than as "surprisingly, we find..." or "counter to expectations."
- Hedge words doing this work across the sample are consistently plain connectives — "however," "but," "notwithstanding," "if anything," "although," "despite" — rather than evaluative adjectives ("surprising," "striking," "puzzling"). This is a specific, actionable stylistic data point.

### Gaps
- This is the weakest-evidenced of the five questions: the sample of 8 did not happen to include a paper whose single headline result is a flat sign reversal of an earlier, separately published finding (the user's paper's exact situation). Searches for Fiscal Studies abstracts using "surprisingly," "counter to expectations," or "reverses" turned up no confirmed Fiscal Studies hits (only non-journal or unrelated results), so it remains unconfirmed whether the journal has ever run an abstract with explicit surprise-language — this should be treated as "no evidence found," not "confirmed absent."
